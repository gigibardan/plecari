The price of a flight can vary by **200–300%** for the same route depending on when and how you search. A Bucharest → Barcelona can cost €55 at 7 weeks out, €95 at 3 weeks, and €180 with 5 days to go. Same plane, same seat — completely different price.

It is not luck or magic. There are specific, documented methods — tested against real 2026 data — that consistently reduce what you pay by 20–50%. This guide covers all of them, from the fundamentals (date flexibility, alerts) to advanced techniques (virtual interlining, error fares, real baggage comparisons). With concrete prices and examples for European travellers.

## 1. Be flexible on dates — the single biggest saving

If you could choose only one method, this is it. The price difference between days of the week is not 5–10% — it is 30–50% on the same routes.

### Which days are cheapest

According to KAYAK's analysis of 917 million fares (January 2025 – March 2026):

| Departure day | Price level | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tuesday** | Cheapest | Minimum demand — neither business nor leisure |
| **Wednesday** | Very cheap | Same pattern, avoided by most travellers |
| **Saturday morning** | Cheap | Early departure = avoided by families |
| Monday | Medium-expensive | Business travel (start of work week) |
| Thursday | Expensive | Early departure for long weekends |
| **Friday evening** | Expensive | Maximum demand — weekend + business |
| **Sunday** | Most expensive | Everyone returning home |

**The concrete difference:** Tuesday departures are **13–14% cheaper** than Sunday. On a €150 route, that is €20–21 saved. On a €400 intercontinental route, €52–56.

### How to use flexibility in practice

**Google Flights — calendar view:**
1. Search your route (e.g. London → Barcelona)
2. For dates, select "Flexible dates" or "Whole month"
3. The calendar shows daily prices, colour-coded: green = cheap, red = expensive
4. Moving 1–2 days can save €30–80

**Skyscanner — "Cheapest month":**
1. Select your route
2. For departure date, choose "Cheapest month"
3. See which months and days have the lowest prices
4. The "Weekend" vs "Weekday" toggle shows the difference

**Kiwi — "± 3 days":**
1. Search your route on Kiwi.com
2. Enable the "± 3 days" flexibility option on departure and return
3. Kiwi searches all combinations (49 variants) and sorts by price

### Cheapest vs most expensive months

| Period | Price level | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **January–February** | Cheapest | Post-holidays, minimum demand |
| March (first half) | Cheap | Before Easter holidays |
| April–May | Medium | Shoulder season — good value for money |
| **June** | Expensive | Start of summer season |
| **July–August** | Most expensive | Peak season, school holidays |
| **September–October** | Cheap | Shoulder season — best quality-to-price ratio |
| November | Cheap | Off-season (exception: Black Friday deals) |
| **December** | Expensive | Winter holidays |

**Concrete tip:** a flight in September costs on average **20–40% less** than in July–August, while the weather is still warm in Mediterranean destinations (Greece, Spain, Italy, Croatia). Same hotel, same beach, half-price ticket.

### Avoid peak periods

The most expensive times of the year:
- **Easter holidays** (April): prices 30–50% above normal
- **Early July** (school holidays begin): demand explosion
- **August bank holidays**: extended weekends, peak pricing
- **Christmas and New Year** (20 December – 5 January): the most expensive days of the year
- **Long weekends** (May Day, national holidays): increased demand on short routes

If you can depart 3–5 days before or after these periods, you save significantly.

## 2. Set price alerts — let the algorithm work for you

Flight prices change **multiple times per day**. A ticket can drop by 30% for a few hours, then return to its previous price. No human can monitor this manually — but alerts can.

### Google Flights — the best for price tracking

Google Flights is the strongest monitoring tool in 2026:

1. Search your route (e.g. London → Athens, 15–22 September)
2. Click the **bell icon** or "Track prices"
3. Google emails you automatically when the price drops significantly
4. On the results page, you see a **price history graph** — it shows whether the current price is "typical," "high" or "low" for that route

**Why it is the best:** Google has access to the most pricing data. Their prediction algorithm shows with high accuracy whether the price will rise or fall in the coming days.

**Google Flights Price Guarantee:** on flights marked with the "Price Guarantee" badge, if the price drops after you book, Google refunds the difference automatically — no forms, no claims. The limit is approximately $500 per traveller. It works primarily on US domestic flights and selectively on international routes, but when the badge appears, it is a genuine advantage.

### Skyscanner — alerts + "Everywhere"

Skyscanner has two unique strengths:

**Price alerts:** work similarly to Google Flights — set the route, receive notifications on drops. Maximum value on routes where the cheapest ticket comes from a carrier Google does not index perfectly (small low-cost airlines, local agencies).

**The "Everywhere" function:** you do not know where you want to go, but you want the cheapest flight from your city? Skyscanner is the only engine that does this well. Select your origin, type "Everywhere" as the destination, and see all destinations sorted by price — with prices starting at €19–29 for European routes.

### Kiwi — alerts + Nomad

**Kiwi** has a good alert system, but its standout feature is **Nomad**: if you want to visit multiple cities and your dates are truly flexible, Nomad finds the cheapest combination of routes. Example: London → Prague → Berlin → Amsterdam → London, with the cheapest flights at each stage.

### When to set alerts

- **3 months before** departure: start monitoring
- **6–8 weeks before**: the window when the best prices tend to appear
- **Act quickly**: when you receive a drop alert, check and book — good offers last hours, not days

**The core rule:** you are not trying to predict the perfect price. Set the alert, wait for a price in the **lower range** of what you have observed (the Google Flights graph helps), and when it appears — act.

## 3. Book at the right time — the optimal booking window

There is no magic moment, but there are windows that consistently produce the best prices. CheapAir analysed 917 million fares and identified clear patterns.

### Optimal booking windows (2026)

| Flight type | Optimal window | Too early | Too late |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Short European** (2–3 hours) | **4–8 weeks** ahead | 3+ months (prices not yet set) | Under 2 weeks (maximum price) |
| **Medium European** (3–5 hours) | **6–10 weeks** ahead | 4+ months | Under 3 weeks |
| **Intercontinental** | **2–3 months** ahead | 6+ months | Under 1 month |
| **Peak season** (July–August) | **As early as possible** | No such thing as too early | Everything is too late |

### Why not "too early"

When you book 4–5 months ahead for a European flight, airlines have not yet started optimising seat fill. The initial price is "standard" — not the highest, but not the lowest either. Discounts come when airlines see the flight is not filling as projected, at 4–8 weeks out.

### Why not "too late"

Under 2 weeks, remaining seats are few. The airline's pricing algorithm knows that anyone searching now probably **needs** to fly (urgency, business) and increases the price. It is supply and demand in its purest form.

### The exception: flash sales and exceptional prices

If you see a clearly exceptional price at any point (even 4 months ahead), **take it**. The rules above are statistical averages — a 50% flash discount at 3 months out beats waiting for the 6-week window.

How to recognise an exceptional price:
- Google Flights shows "Low price" on the history graph
- It is 30%+ below what you have observed in your alerts
- It appears on deal sites (Secret Flying, Fly4Free)

### The 28–35 day rule

For standard intra-European flights, the data shows that **28–35 days** before departure is the window with the best balance of price and availability. It is not an absolute rule, but it is a solid reference point.

## 4. Compare alternative airports — differences of 30–50%

Flying from a secondary airport can save 30–50% on the same journey. The trade-off is transfer time, but the calculation often makes sense.

### Alternative airports at destination

| Going to… | The "official" airport | Cheaper alternative | Typical saving |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Paris** | CDG (Charles de Gaulle) | Beauvais (BVA) — Wizz/Ryanair hub | 20–40% |
| **London** | Heathrow (LHR) | Stansted (STN), Luton (LTN), Gatwick (LGW) | 25–50% |
| **Milan** | Malpensa (MXP) | Bergamo Orio al Serio (BGY) | 30–50% |

| **Berlin** | Brandenburg (BER) | Wrocław (WRO) — 3.5h by train | 20–35% |

### The real calculation: cheap flight + expensive transfer = zero savings?

The alternative airport is cheaper on the ticket, but you still need to reach your actual destination. The correct calculation:

**Example: Paris Beauvais vs CDG:**
- Ticket via Beauvais: €39 (Wizz Air)
- Ticket via CDG: €89 (Air France)
- Transfer Beauvais → central Paris: bus €17, 75 minutes
- Transfer CDG → central Paris: RER €11.80, 35 minutes
- **Real difference:** €89 + €11.80 = €100.80 vs €39 + €17 = €56 → saving of €44.80

**Example: London Stansted vs Heathrow:**
- Ticket via Stansted: €29 (Ryanair)
- Ticket via Heathrow: €109 (BA)
- Transfer Stansted → central London: Stansted Express £22 (€26), 47 minutes
- Transfer Heathrow → central London: Elizabeth Line £12.80 (€15), 35 minutes
- **Real difference:** €109 + €15 = €124 vs €29 + €26 = €55 → saving of €69

**Example: Milan Bergamo vs Malpensa:**
- Ticket via Bergamo: €25 (Ryanair)
- Ticket via Malpensa: €75 (easyJet)
- Transfer Bergamo → Milano Centrale: bus €7, 60 minutes
- Transfer Malpensa → Milano Centrale: Malpensa Express €13, 50 minutes
- **Real difference:** €75 + €13 = €88 vs €25 + €7 = €32 → saving of €56

### How to compare airports in Google Flights

1. In the origin field, click "+"
2. Add multiple airports (e.g. LHR, STN, LTN, LGW)
3. Google shows the cheapest ticket from each airport
4. At the destination, you can do the same — add alternative airports

**Always calculate total cost:** alternative airport saving minus transfer cost (bus, train, taxi to your actual destination). Sometimes the "cheap" option ends up equal to the direct one. Sometimes it saves €100.

### What is "virtual interlining" and how it can save hundreds

Kiwi.com does something the others do not: it combines separate flights from different airlines into a single journey.

**Real example:**
- London → Tokyo direct: €900+
- London → Istanbul (Pegasus): €60
- Istanbul → Tokyo (Turkish Airlines): €380
- **Total through Kiwi: €440** — half the price

Kiwi connects these flights automatically. But watch the **connection risk**: if the first flight is delayed and you miss the second, you have two separate bookings — the second airline has no obligation to wait.

**Kiwi protection:** Kiwi offers "Kiwi Guarantee" (supplementary insurance) that rebooks you on another flight if you miss the connection. It costs extra but provides real protection. From 2026, Kiwi launched "Disruption Protection with Instant Credit" — you receive instant credit for rebooking.

### What is a "Hacker Fare" on KAYAK

KAYAK checks whether two separate one-way tickets from different airlines are cheaper than a round-trip from the same airline. Example:

- London → Milan round-trip with easyJet: €89
- London → Milan one-way Ryanair: €29
- Milan → London one-way Wizz Air: €35
- **KAYAK Hacker Fare: €64** — saving of €25

The risk is minimal (each ticket is a separate booking, but on distinct flights), and the saving is 10–25% on average.

## 8. Check the airline's own website — the final price matters

Search engines are excellent for **discovery** — they find the cheapest flight from 50+ carriers. But the actual booking can sometimes be cheaper directly on the airline's site.

### Why prices differ

1. **OTA commission**: Skyscanner, Kiwi, Momondo redirect to OTAs (Online Travel Agencies) that add a commission of €5–25 per ticket
2. **Lower direct prices**: some low-cost carriers (Ryanair in particular) display higher prices on aggregators to offset commission
3. **Exclusive offers**: airlines sometimes run promotions only on their own website or mobile app

### The efficient booking routine

1. **Discover** on Google Flights or Skyscanner — find the cheapest flight
2. **Note** the airline, date, time, exact route
3. **Open a new tab** with the airline's direct website
4. **Search the same route** directly — compare the total price (including taxes, baggage, seat)
5. **Book on whichever is cheapest** — direct or through the OTA

This process adds 3–5 minutes but regularly saves €10–25 per ticket.

### Mobile apps — sometimes cheaper

Wizz Air and Ryanair sometimes offer prices €2–5 lower in the mobile app than on the website. Wizz Air also has **Wizz Discount Club** (€29.99/year) which gives €5–10 off each ticket + priority + cabin bag — worthwhile if you fly Wizz more than 4–5 times per year.

## 9. Error fares and flash sales — how to catch them

Error fares (mistake fares) are tickets sold at prices far below normal due to a system error — a decimal point in the wrong place, a currency conversion mistake, or a pricing error when launching a new route.

### How real are they

Error fares are **rare but real**. Documented examples:
- Intercontinental business class tickets at €200–400 instead of €3,000+
- European flights at €1–15 (yes, under fifteen euros) due to a tax error
- New routes launched with test prices that remain active for several hours

Average savings on error fares: **€300–1,200 per ticket**. But do not rely on them — they are a bonus when they appear, not a planning strategy.

### Where to find them

| Source | Type | How it works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Secret Flying** | Free website | Lists error fares and deals daily, including from Eastern Europe |
| **Fly4Free** | Free website | European focus, lists flash deals and error fares |
| **Jack's Flight Club** | Newsletter (free + paid) | Sends alerts when exceptional deals appear — premium (£39/year) receives alerts first |
| **Going (formerly Scott's Cheap Flights)** | Newsletter (free + paid) | Similar to Jack's, premium ($49/year) offers error fares and priority access |
| **Google Flights alerts** | Free | Catches error fares indirectly — any sudden price drop appears in your alert |

### How to act on an error fare

1. **Book immediately** — error fares last hours, not days. The airline corrects the error quickly
2. **Do not call the airline** to confirm the price — this draws attention to the error
3. **Pay with a card** that offers payment protection
4. **Wait 24–48 hours** — if the airline cancels the booking, you get your money back
5. **Do not book anything else** (hotel, car, activities) until you have final flight confirmation

### Can the airline cancel an error fare?

Yes, airlines have the legal right to cancel tickets sold at incorrect prices. Most European airlines rarely do this (it is bad PR), but it is not guaranteed. EU regulation does not oblige airlines to honour obvious pricing errors.

**Rule:** book error fares, but do not plan a holiday around them until you have a confirmed ticket.

## 10. Avoid the mistakes that make you overpay

### Mistake 1: Panic booking — "Only 3 seats left!"

"Only 3 seats left at this price!" — may be true, but it is also one of the travel industry's oldest tactics. Prices fluctuate daily. A flight showing "3 seats" at €120 today may show 10 seats tomorrow at €95 — or may indeed sell out. The problem is that **you cannot know**.

**What to do:** if you have researched the route, you know the price is good (alerts and the Google Flights history confirm it), and the dates work — book it. If not — set an alert and come back. Panic booking without comparison costs 15–25% more on average.

### Mistake 2: Searching on only one engine

No search engine has **all** flights at **all** prices. If you only check Google Flights, you miss the offers Skyscanner finds (small low-cost carriers). If you only check Skyscanner, you miss the price prediction from Google.

**Rule:** minimum 2 engines, plus the airline's direct website.

### Mistake 3: Ignoring baggage when comparing

Detailed above — a €29 ticket with €45 baggage is not cheaper than a €69 ticket with baggage included. It seems obvious, but most travellers compare only the base fare.

### Mistake 4: Buying baggage at the airport

Baggage bought at the airport costs **2–3 times more** than online:

| When you buy baggage | Wizz Air (20 kg) | Ryanair (20 kg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| At booking online | €13–35 | €25 |
| Later, online | €25–55 | €30 |
| At the airport | €60+ | €40–50 |

### Mistake 5: Not using a miles card

If you fly regularly, a loyalty card (or a bank card with miles) can accumulate enough miles for a free flight per year. Relevant programmes:

- **Wizz Air Wizz Discount Club**: €29.99/year, €5–10 discount per ticket + priority + cabin bag
- **Star Alliance (Lufthansa, Turkish, LOT, Austrian)**: miles accumulated on every flight, transferable between airlines
- **SkyTeam (KLM, Air France, ITA Airways)**: shared loyalty programme
- **oneworld (British Airways, Iberia, Finnair)**: Avios points, excellent for short-haul redemptions

### Mistake 6: Booking by phone instead of online

Airlines (especially low-cost) add a **booking fee** if you call the call centre instead of booking online: €15–25 per ticket at Wizz Air, €20 at Ryanair. The exception is if you have a technical problem on the website — you can then request a fee waiver.

### Mistake 7: Ignoring the multi-city option

If you are visiting multiple cities, do not book separate tickets. The **multi-city** option in Google Flights or Skyscanner can be **10–25% cheaper** than individual tickets, because it optimises routes.

Example:
- London → Barcelona (€55) + Barcelona → Rome (€35) + Rome → London (€45) = €135 separate
- Multi-city London → Barcelona → Rome → London = €109 (saving of €26)

## 11. Advanced techniques — for experienced travellers

### Hidden city ticketing — legal but risky

Hidden city ticketing means buying a ticket with a connection and getting off at the connection point, without taking the final flight. Example: a ticket London → Munich → Paris may be cheaper than London → Munich direct, if Paris is a cheap destination.

**Real risks:**
- Works **only one-way** — if you do not show up for a segment, the airline cancels the rest of the booking
- **No hold luggage** — bags go to the final destination, not the connection
- Airlines **prohibit** this in their terms and conditions — repeated use can cost you your loyalty account
- Some airlines have sued Skiplagged (the site that facilitates this)

**Potential saving:** 20–60%. **Recommendation:** only for experienced travellers, only one-way, only with carry-on.

### One-way vs round-trip — when is it cheaper

At low-cost carriers (Wizz, Ryanair, easyJet), **there is no difference** — each flight is priced separately. You can buy outbound with one airline and return with another without penalty.

At full-service carriers (Lufthansa, BA, Turkish), the round-trip ticket is usually cheaper than two one-ways. But not always — check both options.

## 13. Flights for families — the complete calculation

When flying as a family (2 adults + 1–2 children), the calculation changes — and so does the winner.

### Family tips

1. **Book early** — families with small children need adjacent seats, which sell first
2. **Check the pushchair policy** — all carriers allow a pushchair free of charge (gate check-in or counter)
3. **Calculate the price with the shopping cart**, not from the headline
4. **Ryanair Regular is surprisingly good** for families on short routes, as it includes a 20 kg hold bag and seat selection
5. **Full-service carriers become competitive** for families needing 3–4 hold bags — at that point the included baggage tips the calculation

## 14. Pre-booking checklist

Before you press "Book," check these points:

### Pre-booking checklist

- [ ] Compared on at least **2 search engines** (Google Flights + Skyscanner/Kiwi)
- [ ] Checked the price on the **airline's own website**
- [ ] Compared the **TOTAL price** (ticket + baggage + seat), not just the base fare
- [ ] Checked **alternative airports** (origin and destination)
- [ ] Checked whether **shifting 1–2 days** on dates drops the price
- [ ] Checked whether a **connecting flight** is significantly cheaper
- [ ] Checked **cancellation and change conditions**
- [ ] Bought **baggage online** (not at the airport)
- [ ] Checked **travel documents** needed (passport, visa, ETIAS if applicable)

### Travel documents — what to check

- **EU/Schengen:** national ID card or passport
- **UK (post-Brexit):** passport required
- **Turkey:** passport or national ID card (check validity — minimum 6 months on entry for some nationalities)
- **USA:** passport + ESTA (online application, $21, approved within hours)
- **ETIAS (from 2026):** new authorisation system for EU citizens travelling to non-Schengen countries with visa waiver agreements — check if active for your destination

## 15. Do not be manipulated by artificial urgency

"Only 3 seats left at this price!" — may be true, but it is often a pressure tactic. Prices change, but they do not disappear.

### How urgency messaging works

Airlines and OTAs use several tactics:
- **Scarcity** ("3 seats left"): may be true, but "at this price" — the price changes anyway
- **Countdown timer**: creates artificial pressure — the price does not expire, the timer does
- **"X people looking now"**: often exaggerated or irrelevant
- **Price increase on second search**: less common in 2026, but some OTAs still do it

### The right response

1. **If you have already researched the route** and the price is good — book it. That is not panic, it is an informed decision.
2. **If you have not researched** — set an alert and come back. A good price that disappears today may return tomorrow.
3. **Never book hotel/car/activities** before you have a confirmed ticket — especially on error fares.

## Putting it together: a realistic booking process

1. Set your destination and rough travel window (± 3 days on each end)
2. Search on Google Flights and Skyscanner with flexible dates enabled — look at the cheapest-day calendar
3. Set a price alert on Google Flights for your route
4. Check Kiwi if the route has no direct flight or the direct is expensive
5. When a price appears that is in the bottom 25% of what you have been seeing — book it
6. Before confirming, open the airline's direct website and compare the total price
7. If booking well in advance, also search for alternative airports at both ends

This process takes one active hour and then passive monitoring via email alerts. Most people who consistently pay less for flights are not doing anything magical — they are just not booking in a rush.
