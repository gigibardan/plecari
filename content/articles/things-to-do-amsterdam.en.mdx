Amsterdam does not try to be Paris. It does not compete with Rome on monuments or Barcelona on beaches. It is entirely, stubbornly itself — a city built on water, on tolerance, and on the conviction that beauty is best appreciated from a bicycle seat or a canal boat going nowhere in particular.

Here is how to actually experience it.

## The Rijksmuseum — one painting justifies the entire trip

The Rijksmuseum houses one of the great collections of Western art, but most people come for a single work: Rembrandt's *The Night Watch* (1642), the largest and most technically ambitious painting of the Dutch Golden Age. It occupies its own room, 3.6 metres tall, and it rewards being seen in person in a way that no reproduction prepares you for.

Beyond Rembrandt, Vermeer's *The Milkmaid* and *Woman Reading a Letter* are nearby — smaller, quieter, and if anything more astonishing up close. Vermeer painted only 34 works in his lifetime; the Rijksmuseum has four of them.

Practical: arrive when it opens (9 AM). The Night Watch room gets crowded by 10:30. Book tickets online (€22, timed entry) — the queue for walk-ups can be 45 minutes. The museum takes 2–3 hours minimum.

## Anne Frank House — book immediately, not when you arrive

The Anne Frank House is where Anne Frank and her family hid for 761 days during the Nazi occupation, before being discovered and deported in August 1944. Anne died in Bergen-Belsen concentration camp in February 1945, three months before liberation. She was 15.

The visit moves through the actual hidden annex — the rooms where eight people lived in complete silence during daytime for over two years. It is a serious, deeply moving experience that most visitors describe as one of the most affecting things they have ever done.

The practical reality: **tickets sell out 6–8 weeks ahead in summer.** There are no walk-up tickets. If you arrive without a booking, you cannot enter. Book on annefrank.org immediately after confirming your Amsterdam dates. €16 adults, free under 10.

## Van Gogh Museum — the complete story in one building

The Van Gogh Museum holds the largest collection of Van Gogh's work in the world — 200 paintings and 500 drawings, covering his entire career. It is organised chronologically, so you trace the full arc: the dark palette of his Dutch years, the transformation in Paris after he discovered Impressionism, the exploding colour and movement of Arles, and the final works from Saint-Rémy.

*Sunflowers* is here. So is *The Bedroom*, the self-portraits, *Almond Blossom*, and *Wheatfield with Crows* — painted three weeks before his death.

Book in advance (€22, timed entry). The museum is across Museumplein from the Rijksmuseum and the combination is a natural full-day programme.

## Canal boat tour — the city from water level

Amsterdam has more canals than Venice and more bridges than any city in Europe (1,500). The canal ring — the UNESCO-listed Grachtengordel — was planned and built in the early 17th century as the world's first large-scale urban planning project. It has barely changed since.

A canal boat tour shows you a completely different Amsterdam: the backs of the canal houses, the houseboats (2,500 of them, many inhabited), the bridges at water level, and the scale of the Golden Age merchant architecture that defines the city.

Options range from a 75-minute open boat tour (€15–18) to private evening cruises with drinks (€40–60). The Prins Hendrikkade departure point near Central Station has the most options. Evening tours in summer, with the canal house lights reflecting on the water, are genuinely spectacular.

## The Jordaan — the neighbourhood that explains Amsterdam

The Jordaan was built in the 17th century as a working-class district west of the main canals, and it remains the most livable part of the city. Narrow streets, houseboats, independent galleries, brown cafés (bruine kroegen — the traditional Dutch pub with dark wood, candles, and no food beyond bitterballen), and some of the best cheese shops in the country.

Spend a morning walking without a destination: Bloemgracht, Egelantiersgracht, the Noordermarkt (Saturday market, 9 AM–4 PM, organic food and vintage), and Elandsgracht. The Anne Frank House is on the eastern edge of the Jordaan — combine them.

There are no major attractions here. That is the point.

## The Amsterdam coffee shop scene — a practical guide

Amsterdam's tolerance policy (*gedoogbeleid*) for cannabis has been in effect since the late 1970s. The result: approximately 150 licensed coffee shops across the city where adults can purchase and consume cannabis legally within certain limits.

**The legal framework (2026):** Cannabis is officially illegal in the Netherlands but possession of up to 5g and purchase at licensed coffee shops is formally tolerated. This is a specific Dutch legal category — not legalisation, but prosecutorial non-enforcement for personal use.

**Rules that apply:**
- You must be 18 or older (ID checked at entrance)
- Maximum purchase: 5g per person per visit
- Consumption inside the coffee shop only
- Coffee shops cannot sell alcohol (it is one or the other — Dutch law)
- No smoking tobacco mixed with cannabis is allowed in many coffee shops under indoor smoking laws
- Outdoor smoking of cannabis is restricted in most central areas since 2023 and enforced in tourist zones

**What you will actually find:** Products range from joints (pre-rolled, €4–8) to loose cannabis sold by gram (€8–15/g depending on variety), space cakes (edibles, €5–8, with highly variable and delayed effects — treat carefully), and hash. Most coffee shops have a menu card.

**Recommended coffee shops for first-timers:**
- **Paradox** (Jordaan) — small, friendly, low-key local atmosphere, the easiest for beginners
- **The Dampkring** (Haarlemmerstraat) — appeared in *Ocean's Twelve*, consistently high quality
- **Boerejongens** (multiple locations) — known for quality and transparency, the most "professional" operation
- **Grey Area** (Jordaan) — American-owned, tiny (8 people), cult following for quality

**Practical notes:** Do not buy from street dealers (illegal, dangerous, often synthetic products). Edibles (space cakes) hit significantly harder and slower than smoked cannabis — if you are new to them, eat half and wait 90 minutes before deciding to eat more. Mixing with alcohol strongly amplifies both effects.

This is legal tourism with centuries of local context. Treat it with the same adult common sense you would apply to anything else.

## Cycling in Amsterdam — the only way to move like a local

Amsterdam has 800km of dedicated bicycle lanes and 900,000 bicycles for 900,000 inhabitants. Cycling is not a tourist activity here — it is infrastructure. The city was designed (and redesigned in the 1970s) around the bicycle, and it shows.

Renting a bicycle fundamentally changes the experience. You stop being a tourist navigating crowds and start being someone who lives here, at least for a day.

**Where to rent:**
- **MacBike** (Central Station and 3 other locations) — the tourist standard, €15–18/day
- **Donkey Republic** (app-based) — €12/day, unlock via smartphone
- **Yellow Bike** — guided tours available

**The rules locals actually follow:**
- Bike lanes are sacred. Never walk in them. They look like pavements but they are not.
- Lock your bicycle with two locks (the D-lock through the frame AND a cable through the wheel)
- At traffic lights, cyclists often continue carefully — watch what the person ahead does
- Give way to trams — always

**Where to cycle:** the Jordaan, the Vondelpark (the central park, also a destination in itself), along the Amstel river south of the city, and out to NDSM Wharf (ferry from Central Station, free) for a very different, industrial Amsterdam.

## Markets worth going out of your way for

**Albert Cuyp Market** (Pijp neighbourhood, Monday–Saturday, 9 AM–5 PM) — Amsterdam's largest street market, 260 stalls, everything from raw herring to fabric to electronics. The *haringbroodje* (raw herring sandwich, €4) from one of the fish stalls is mandatory. Eat it the Dutch way: hold by the tail, tilt your head back.

**Noordermarkt** (Jordaan, Saturday 9 AM–4 PM) — organic food, artisan produce, and a separate section for vintage and antiques. More relaxed than Albert Cuyp, better for browsing.

**Waterlooplein Flea Market** (Monday–Saturday) — Amsterdam's oldest market, vintage clothing, second-hand books, curiosities. Increasingly touristy but still worth 30 minutes.

## The I Amsterdam City Card — does it pay off?

The I Amsterdam City Card (€65/24h, €85/48h, €100/72h) includes:
- Unlimited GVB public transport
- Free entry to 70+ museums including Rijksmuseum and Van Gogh Museum
- Free canal boat tour
- Discounts at restaurants, shops, and attractions

**The maths for 48 hours:** Rijksmuseum (€22) + Van Gogh (€22) + canal tour (€17) + transport (€19 for 2-day pass) = €80 in paid items alone. The 48h card costs €85. If you add the Amsterdam Museum, EYE Film Institute, or a Heineken Experience, it pays for itself clearly.

If you are only visiting for one day and doing just two museums — buy individually.

## What Amsterdam is not

Amsterdam is not a party city in the sense that some visitors expect. The coffee shops and the red-light district exist, and they are part of the city's identity, but they are a small corner of a place that is genuinely one of the most architecturally, culturally, and humanly interesting cities in Europe.

The visitors who get the most out of Amsterdam are the ones who rent a bicycle on day one and spend an afternoon getting slightly lost in the Jordaan. The ones who book the Anne Frank House far in advance and go with the understanding that it is not just a museum. The ones who sit in a brown café in the afternoon with a jenever (Dutch gin) and watch the boats go past.

That Amsterdam — quiet, precise, tolerant, and completely itself — is worth every euro of the 16% tourist tax.
