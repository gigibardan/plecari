Florence stops you mid-stride. You turn a corner on a street that seems like any other and the Duomo fills the sky in front of you, appearing with no warning, impossible in its scale and beauty. You step into a church that looks unremarkable from the outside and find a Botticelli altarpiece lit by afternoon sun, with no one else in the room. This happens repeatedly in Florence, and it never stops being remarkable.

The city of roughly 360,000 people packs the most concentrated collection of Renaissance art and architecture on Earth into a compact historic centre that most visitors cover on foot. It is also a city that rewards depth: the same places look completely different depending on whether you know the story behind them. The Dome is beautiful from the outside. It becomes extraordinary when you understand that its builder invented the methods of its construction as he went, keeping them secret from everyone, including his own co-superintendent, in case he died or was replaced.

This guide covers everything: the major sites with the detail they deserve, the hidden places most visitors never find, the food you should eat, and the practical information that makes the difference between a frustrating visit and an unforgettable one.

## When to Visit — The Honest Seasonal Guide

**April–June:** The best months for a first visit. Temperatures 18–26°C, wildflowers in the Tuscan countryside, the city's rose gardens in bloom (Giardino delle Rose above the Oltrarno is extraordinary in May), and crowds manageable outside of Easter weekend. Book the Uffizi and Dome 2–3 weeks ahead.

**September–October:** The second golden window. Temperatures 20–28°C in September, vineyard harvests beginning in October (the smell of fermenting Chianti fills the Oltrarno in October), tourists thinning after mid-September. The truffle season starts in San Miniato (white truffle, October–December) — some of the best pasta dishes of the year appear on menus.

**July–August:** Beautiful but intense. 30–38°C, crowded queues at every major site, museums and galleries packed by 10:00. The Dome sells out 6 weeks ahead. If you visit in August, book everything the moment you confirm dates, and schedule outdoor sightseeing for early morning.

**November–March:** Cold (5–12°C), many Tuscan day-trip destinations closed, but the city itself is atmospheric and completely navigable. The Uffizi and Accademia are almost walk-in accessible. The Christmas markets around Piazza Santa Croce are genuinely good.

## The Neighbourhoods — How Florence Is Divided

Florence's historic centre is more compact than almost any major European capital. Santa Maria Novella station to the Uffizi is 15 minutes on foot. The Duomo to Piazzale Michelangelo is 30 minutes. Most visitors never need public transport.

### Centro Storico — The Renaissance Core

The area between the Duomo, the Uffizi, Piazza della Repubblica, and Piazza Santa Croce. The streets here — Via dei Calzaiuoli, Via Porta Rossa, Borgo degli Albizi — are medieval in layout and contain the densest concentration of art and architecture.

**The problem with staying too close to the Duomo:** restaurants and cafés within 200 metres of the Duomo and Uffizi charge tourist prices for mediocre food. Walk two streets in any direction and the quality improves sharply.

### San Lorenzo and the Mercato Centrale

North of the Duomo, the San Lorenzo market area is Florence's most local-feeling central neighbourhood. The Mercato Centrale (the iron-and-glass covered market, open from 07:00) has the best fresh produce, charcuterie, and cheese in the city. The upstairs food hall (open until midnight) is excellent for quick affordable lunches. The Sant'Ambrogio market, 10 minutes east, is even more local.

### Santa Croce

The eastern quadrant of the historic centre, centring on the Basilica di Santa Croce — the "Pantheon of Florence," where Michelangelo, Galileo, Machiavelli, and Ghiberti are buried. The neighbourhood around it, particularly Via dei Benci and the streets toward the Arno, is where many locals eat and drink — less touristic than the Duomo area.

### The Oltrarno — Where Florence Actually Lives

"Oltrarno" means "beyond the Arno" — the neighbourhood south of the river, reached by crossing the Ponte Vecchio or any of the other bridges. This is Florence for anyone who wants to go beyond the brochure. Artisan workshops producing leather goods, bookbinding, gilded frames, furniture restoration, and custom jewellery line the streets. The bars and restaurants on Piazza Santo Spirito are full of students and professionals, not tourists.

Sub-areas: **San Frediano** (the most working-class, most authentic, gentrifying slowly — Borgo San Frediano named one of Europe's coolest streets recently); **San Niccolò** (quieter, more residential, medieval gate Torre San Niccolò open for evening climbs in summer); **Piazza Santo Spirito** (the neighbourhood's social centre, morning market on weekdays, excellent nightlife).

The Oltrarno holds: Palazzo Pitti (five museums), Boboli Gardens, Bardini Garden, Forte di Belvedere, Brancacci Chapel, Piazzale Michelangelo, San Miniato al Monte. Cross the Arno and spend at least a full afternoon here.

## The Major Sites — With the Detail They Deserve

### The Duomo Complex — A Story of Genius, Rivalry, and Secrecy

The complex of Santa Maria del Fiore — cathedral, dome, bell tower (Campanile di Giotto), Baptistery, and the Opera del Duomo museum — is the heart of Florence and one of the most extraordinary concentrations of architecture and art from any period.

**The Cathedral (free entry):** Construction began in 1296 under Arnolfo di Cambio, continued under Giotto (who designed the bell tower) and Francesco Talenti, and concluded with Brunelleschi's dome in 1436 — a building project of 140 years. The white Carrara marble, green Prato serpentine, and red Maremma marble of the polychrome facade (completed 1887 in Gothic style that matches the original intent) create a surface that changes character entirely under different light conditions.

**Brunelleschi's Dome — the whole story:**

In 1418, after 122 years of construction, the cathedral had a vast octagonal hole where the dome should be. The problem: the opening was 42 metres wide, the planned dome was to rise 55 metres above the drum, and there was no known technique for building it without wooden centring — temporary scaffolding that normally supports an arch or dome during construction. But no tree in Europe was tall enough to span 42 metres, and even if the timber could be sourced, the weight would be unsustainable.

Filippo Brunelleschi, a goldsmith who had never built anything of significance, entered the competition for the dome commission. His fellow competitor was Lorenzo Ghiberti — the most celebrated Florentine artist of the moment, who had already beaten Brunelleschi in the 1401 competition for the Baptistery bronze doors (a loss that stung Brunelleschi so deeply he left Florence for Rome with his friend Donatello, where they spent years studying ancient Roman construction).

Brunelleschi won the dome commission, but the Opera del Duomo (the building authority) didn't fully trust him. They appointed Ghiberti as co-superintendent — a calculated insult to both men. The two rivals were forced to work together. Brunelleschi's response was to keep his methods completely secret: he told workers only what they needed for the next step, never revealing the overall plan, apparently fearing that if his techniques were known, the authorities might replace him.

His solution was a double shell — two domes, one inside the other, self-supporting through interlocking masonry rather than wooden centring. The inner dome provides structural integrity; the outer dome gives the dramatic silhouette visible from across Tuscany. The brickwork follows a herringbone pattern (a technique he invented or adapted) that distributes stress around the circular form. He built custom cranes and hoisting machinery to lift materials to the construction level, since the traditional method of workers carrying materials up ladders was impossible at this scale.

When Brunelleschi feigned illness in 1434 and refused to come to the site, everything stopped. Ghiberti, who had been present for 14 years without understanding the method, couldn't continue. (Some historians believe Ghiberti engineered Brunelleschi's arrest on a guild technicality the same year — a moment of bureaucratic revenge.) The authorities reinstated Brunelleschi immediately.

Ghiberti's assistant, the architect Giovanni da Prato, was so incensed by Brunelleschi's success that he wrote a sonnet calling Brunelleschi "a miserable and imbecile beast" and a "dark, deep wellspring of ignorance" and promised to kill himself if Brunelleschi's plan succeeded. The plan succeeded. Da Prato did not follow through.

The dome was consecrated on 25 March 1436 — the Feast of the Annunciation — by Pope Eugenius IV. Brunelleschi died ten years later, in 1446, and was buried in the cathedral crypt with his "divine intellect" commemorated on a memorial plaque. This was extraordinary — before him, the crypt was reserved for saints.

**The dome climb (463 steps):** You ascend between the two shells of the dome — the narrow staircase runs through the masonry, and at points you can see Vasari and Zuccari's Last Judgement fresco (painted 1572–1579, covering 3,600 m²) from the inside at close range. The view from the lantern at the top: Florence spread across the valley of the Arno, the Tuscan hills, and the red-tile roofscape below. **Book the time slot well in advance** — this sells out faster than any other Florence attraction.

**Baptistery and the Gates of Paradise:**

The Baptistery of San Giovanni (opposite the cathedral's main entrance) is one of Florence's oldest buildings — the exterior octagonal structure dates from the 11th–12th centuries, though the site has been a place of worship since Roman times.

Its three sets of bronze doors are Florence's greatest public artwork. The north doors were cast by Lorenzo Ghiberti after his famous 1401 competition victory. The east doors — made by Ghiberti between 1425 and 1452 after he won a second commission — were described by Michelangelo as "so beautiful they deserve to be the Gates of Paradise." The name stuck.

What's on display on the Baptistery today are high-quality copies. The originals were removed to prevent further weathering and are in the Museo dell'Opera del Duomo — where you can examine them at eye level, close enough to see the individual figures in each gilded panel.

**Museo dell'Opera del Duomo:** The museum that holds the originals of Ghiberti's Gates of Paradise, a Pietà by Michelangelo (the Pietà Bandini — an unfinished work he was making for his own tomb, which he broke in frustration and a student later repaired), Donatello's wooden Magdalene, and Luca della Robbia's original Cantoria (marble choir gallery with singing children). This museum is one of the great art repositories of Florence, and it's dramatically less crowded than the Uffizi or Accademia. Included in the Brunelleschi Pass.

**Ticket options (2026):**
- Cathedral only: Free (queues for security)
- Brunelleschi Pass (dome + baptistery + bell tower + crypt + museum): **€30**, valid 72 hours
- Giotto Pass (everything except dome): **€20**
- Official booking: duomo.firenze.it

### Uffizi Gallery — One of the World's Great Art Museums

The Uffizi (Galleria degli Uffizi) houses the Medici family's art collection — assembled over 200 years by the most powerful patrons in Renaissance history. What this means in practice: an unbroken sequence of masterpieces from Cimabue and Giotto in the 13th century through Botticelli, Leonardo, Michelangelo, and Raphael in the 15th and 16th, and on to Titian, Caravaggio, and Rembrandt. There are approximately 3,000 works in the collection; around 1,500 are on permanent display.

**Room 10–14 — Botticelli:** The rooms that most visitors come specifically to see. The Birth of Venus (1484–1486) and Primavera (1477–1482) are the Uffizi's most famous works, and genuinely extraordinary on a scale that reproduction doesn't prepare you for. The Birth of Venus is nearly 3 metres wide; the figure of Venus emerges from the foam of the sea with an expression that has been analysed for 500 years without definitive interpretation. Primavera — the allegory of spring — contains nine figures, each identifiable through mythological iconography, set against a dark grove. The orange-gold of the citrus fruits, the blue of Mercury's cloak, the translucent gauze of the Graces: Botticelli painted this for Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco de' Medici, probably to hang in a room where it would be studied for meaning, not merely admired for beauty.

**Room 15 — Leonardo da Vinci:** The Annunciation (1472–1475) and the unfinished Adoration of the Magi (1481). The Annunciation was painted when Leonardo was approximately 20, still working in Verrocchio's workshop — the figure of the angel Gabriel has already the characteristic softness of atmosphere that defines Leonardo's mature style.

**Michelangelo's Doni Tondo (Room 35):** The only completed panel painting by Michelangelo. The Holy Family in a circular frame (tondo), with young St John the Baptist and a group of nude male figures in the background. The colours are shocking — acidic green, electric orange — anticipating Mannerism by a generation. Michelangelo and the patron Agnolo Doni famously argued about the price after delivery.

**Caravaggio (Rooms 90–93):** Medusa on a shield (a self-portrait), Bacchus (possibly another self-portrait, the fruit slightly overripe), Sacrifice of Isaac, and the extraordinary Boy Being Bitten by a Lizard — in which the pain and shock on the boy's face is rendered with a psychological realism that no painter before Caravaggio had achieved.

**The Uffizi terrace:** Walk through the corridor connecting the west and east wings and emerge onto a loggia overlooking Piazza della Signoria and the Duomo — a panorama of Florence from a gallery window, free, no extra ticket, available during museum hours.

**Practical:** Entry €25–29 (prices vary by season). Book online at uffizi.it or via GetYourGuide weeks ahead in summer. **Start with Room 2** (Cimabue and Giotto — the revolution that preceded the Renaissance) and work forward chronologically. Allow 2.5–3 hours minimum; 4–5 hours for a comprehensive visit. The museum is slightly east of the main tourist cluster — the piazza outside is quieter than the Duomo area and one of Florence's most beautiful public spaces.

### Galleria dell'Accademia — Michelangelo's David

The Accademia Gallery was founded in 1784 by Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo to teach art students from originals. The collection of 15th and 16th century paintings is genuinely good — but everyone comes for David.

**Michelangelo's David (1501–1504):** 5.17 metres tall, carved from a single block of Carrara marble that had been quarried in 1464 and abandoned for 35 years (because another sculptor had made a preliminary cut that was considered a ruinous mistake — Michelangelo used that cut for the legs). Michelangelo was 26 years old when he received the commission. He worked for two years in near-secrecy; the finished statue was unveiled in 1504 and moved to its original position outside Palazzo Vecchio, where it stood until 1873 (the version outside the Palazzo today is a copy; the original came to the Accademia for conservation).

The proportions are intentionally distorted: David's hands and head are larger than anatomically correct, because the statue was originally intended for a high position on Florence Cathedral's buttresses, where it would be seen from below. Michelangelo corrected for the distortion that viewing angle would create.

The expression — the slightly dilated left eye, the tensed jaw, the veins in the hand — depicts the moment before the confrontation with Goliath, not after. This is new: previous Davids (Donatello's, Verrocchio's) showed the victor after victory. Michelangelo shows the instant of highest psychological tension before the first stone is thrown.

**The Prisoners (Prigioni):** In the corridor leading to David, four unfinished sculptures — figures apparently struggling to emerge from the marble. These were made for the tomb of Pope Julius II (a project that consumed decades of Michelangelo's life and was never completed). They appear to be fighting the stone; the effect is of extraordinary physical and psychological tension. Michelangelo either left them unfinished deliberately or ran out of time. Either way, they are among his most moving works.

**New (2026): Combined ticket Accademia + Bargello:** €26, valid 48 hours. Highly recommended — see the two most important sculpture collections in Florence together.

**Practical:** Entry €16. Book at uffizi.it (Accademia tickets are on the same platform). Open Tuesday–Sunday. Queue without a ticket in summer can be 2 hours.

### The Bargello — Italy's Greatest Renaissance Sculpture Museum

The most underrated major museum in Florence. Built in 1255 as Florence's first government building (the bargello was the chief of police, hence the name), converted to a prison in the 16th century, opened as a museum in 1865. Almost no tourists come here directly — they come to the Uffizi and Accademia. The Bargello opens at 08:15 and is quiet for the first 90 minutes every day.

**What's here:**

**Donatello's bronze David (c.1440–1450):** The first freestanding life-size nude sculpture since antiquity. David in a wide-brimmed hat, standing on Goliath's severed head, with an expression that is ambiguous, unsettling, and unlike anything sculpture had attempted since ancient Greece. Commissioned by Cosimo de' Medici for his private courtyard — this was a private object, not a public monument. The bronze casting alone was a technical achievement of the highest order for its time.

**Michelangelo's Bacchus (1496–1497):** One of his earliest surviving large sculptures, made for the Roman banker Jacopo Galli. A drunk Bacchus, slightly swaying, with a glazed expression and a cup of wine. Behind him, a small satyr stealing grapes from under his arm. The Romans who commissioned it wanted an antique work; they considered it so successful that some believed it actually was ancient.

**The 1401 competition panels:** Both Brunelleschi's and Ghiberti's bronze trial panels for the Baptistery door commission — both depicting the Sacrifice of Isaac — are in the Bargello, side by side. This is rare: you can examine the two competing submissions that changed the history of Renaissance art, in the same room, at the same time.

**Cellini's Perseus model (1545–1554):** The small-scale wax and bronze model Cellini made for the Perseus that stands in the Loggia dei Lanzi in Piazza della Signoria (the finished version is too large and too far away to examine properly). The Bargello model lets you see the extraordinary detail of Cellini's work close up.

**Practical:** Entry €10. Via del Proconsolo 4 (5 minutes from the Uffizi). Open 08:15–13:50 (Tuesday–Saturday, first and third Sunday, second and fourth Monday of each month — check before visiting). Combined with Accademia: €26.

### Piazza della Signoria and the Loggia dei Lanzi

Florence's main civic square has been the political heart of the city since the 13th century. The Palazzo Vecchio (the medieval fortress-palace, now the city hall and a museum) anchors the piazza; a copy of Michelangelo's David stands outside the entrance where the original stood until 1873.

The **Loggia dei Lanzi** — the open-air sculpture gallery on the south side of the piazza — displays some of the most significant large-scale Renaissance bronzes in existence in the open air, for free. Cellini's Perseus Holding the Head of Medusa (1545–1554) and Giambologna's Rape of the Sabine Women (1583) are the highlights. The Perseus in particular — the culmination of Cellini's career, cast in one spectacular pour after repeated earlier failures — is here for anyone to walk up and look at, without a ticket, any time the piazza is open.

**Palazzo Vecchio Museum:** Entry approximately €19. Includes the Hall of the Five Hundred (the largest room in any palace of the Italian Renaissance, covered in frescoes by Vasari — but concealing, according to some scholars, a lost Leonardo da Vinci mural on the wall behind Vasari's paintings), the Study of Francesco I (a tiny jewel-box room entirely decorated in Mannerist panels), and the tower (Torre di Arnolfo) for panoramic views.

## The Oltrarno — Florence's Other Half

Cross any bridge over the Arno and you're in a different city. The Oltrarno is where Florentines actually live, work, and eat — artisan workshops have operated here continuously since the Middle Ages, and unlike the centro storico, the Oltrarno has maintained a functioning neighbourhood rather than becoming entirely tourist-oriented.

### Palazzo Pitti and Boboli Gardens

The Medici's principal residence from 1550, the Palazzo Pitti is a vast Renaissance palace containing five separate museums — the Palatine Gallery (the Medici's private art collection, with Raphael, Titian, Caravaggio, and Rubens hung wall-to-wall in the original rooms), the Royal Apartments, the Gallery of Modern Art, the Silver Museum, and the Carriage Museum.

The **Boboli Gardens** behind the palace — 45,000 m² of terraced Medici gardens with grottoes, fountains, sculptures, and views toward the city — are separately ticketed at €10–13 (or combined with Pitti at €22). A genuine respite from the city, best in the late afternoon.

### Brancacci Chapel — The Sistine Chapel of the Early Renaissance

Inside the Church of Santa Maria del Carmine in San Frediano, the Brancacci Chapel contains frescoes painted between 1424 and 1427 by Masaccio, with later additions by Masolino and Filippino Lippi. Only 30 visitors are allowed in the chapel at a time.

Masaccio was approximately 22 years old when he began these paintings, and he died at 27 — but the Brancacci Chapel frescoes are among the most important works in the history of Western art. For the first time in painting, figures have weight, cast shadows, occupy space convincingly, and display facial expressions of genuine emotional complexity. The Tribute Money — in which Christ, the Apostles, and the tax collector interact in a landscape with actual atmospheric perspective — influenced Botticelli, Leonardo, Michelangelo, and Raphael directly. Michelangelo came to copy the Brancacci Chapel figures and had his nose broken by a fellow student he'd insulted over his sketches. The nose remained crooked for the rest of his life.

Entry: €10. Limited capacity — book in advance (cappellabrancacci.it) to guarantee a slot. Closed Tuesdays.

### Bardini Garden — The Best View in Florence

On Costa San Giorgio, above the San Niccolò neighbourhood: the Bardini Garden is a Baroque-style terraced garden in a hillside position that gives continuous views toward the Duomo, the Arno, and the city. Unlike the crowded Boboli Gardens, Bardini is quiet — there are rarely more than a few dozen visitors at a time. In spring (typically late April to mid-May), the wisteria tunnel — a 100-metre pergola of century-old wisteria — blooms in dense purple and lilac cascades. The perfume is extraordinary. Entry: €10.

**The Caffetteria delle Oblate:** For a free Duomo view, enter the Oblate public library on Via dell'Oriuolo (near the Duomo, behind the cathedral's south side), take the stairs to the rooftop café. Order a coffee (€3) and look directly at the dome from the terrace. Almost no tourists know this exists. No ticket, no booking, open during library hours.

### San Miniato al Monte

A 12th-century Romanesque church on a hilltop above the Oltrarno — one of the finest examples of Romanesque architecture in Tuscany, with an inlaid marble facade in white and green and extraordinary floor mosaics inside. The walk up from the Arno takes approximately 30 minutes (via the Rampe del Poggi from Piazzale Michelangelo, or directly uphill from San Niccolò).

The view from the church terrace is different from Piazzale Michelangelo (from which most people photograph Florence) — quieter, higher, with the Chianti hills visible to the south. The Benedictine monks who still inhabit the adjacent monastery sing Vespers at 17:30 daily. Arriving for Vespers means you hear the plainchant in a medieval space with no amplification while the late-afternoon light turns the city below to copper and gold. Free entry.

## What Florence's Best Visitors Do Differently

**Skip the Firenze Card.** Unless you're planning to visit more than 8–10 museums in 72 hours, the card (€85+) is rarely cost-effective. Calculate your specific museum list and buy individual tickets.

**The Uffizi before 09:00.** The museum opens at 08:15 on weekdays. Visitors with booked first-entry slots see the Botticelli rooms almost alone. By 10:30, those same rooms are impenetrably crowded.

**Avoid restaurants within 200 metres of the Duomo and Uffizi.** This rule is reliable. Two streets in any direction in the Oltrarno, Sant'Ambrogio, or San Lorenzo area gives you local prices and quality. Specific streets: Borgo San Jacopo (Oltrarno), Via dei Serragli (San Frediano), Via dei Macci (Sant'Ambrogio).

**The Bargello at opening.** 08:15, first Sunday of the month when entry is free, is remarkable — you'll have Donatello's David and Michelangelo's Bacchus almost to yourself.

**Eat lunch at Trattoria Mario.** Via Rosina 2, near Mercato Centrale. Open since 1953, no reservations, communal tables, cash only, the menu changes daily. Arrive at 12:00 when it opens — by 12:30 there's a queue outside. Full meal with wine: €15–18.

**Walk across Ponte Vecchio at 07:00.** The bridge (built 1345, the only Florentine bridge the Germans didn't destroy in 1944) is lined with goldsmiths' shops — a function it has served since the 16th century, when the Medici ordered the original butchers and tanners to be evicted for reasons of smell. In the early morning before the shops open, you have it to yourself.

**The Vasari Corridor:** Built in 1565 for Cosimo I de' Medici to walk from the Palazzo Vecchio to Palazzo Pitti without descending to street level, the Corridor runs above the Ponte Vecchio shops and through the Uffizi. It was reopened for visits in 2024 after extensive renovation. Timed tickets required — check uffizi.it for current availability.

## Food and Wine in Florence

### What to Eat

**Lampredotto:** The fourth stomach of a cow, slow-braised, sliced thin, served in a semelle roll with salsa verde and optional spicy sauce. Florence's working-class street food, sold from trippaio carts for €4–5. Find it near Mercato Centrale, Mercato Sant'Ambrogio (the more local market, excellent for breakfast), and Piazza dei Cimatori near Piazza della Signoria.

**Bistecca alla Fiorentina:** The definitive Tuscan dish. A T-bone from Chianina IGP cattle (the huge white breed of the Val di Chiana valley south of Arezzo), grilled over hardwood embers to rare, finished with coarse salt and Tuscan olive oil. Minimum 1 kg, usually 1.2–1.5 kg, priced by weight (€50–65/kg in 2026). For two people. Non-negotiably rare — requesting well-done is a refusal. **Trattoria dall'Oste** (Via Luigi Alamanni) and **Buca Lapi** (Via del Trebbio) are the reliable addresses.

**Pappa al pomodoro:** Tuscan bread-and-tomato soup — stale bread rehydrated with fresh tomatoes, olive oil, and basil. Humble, extraordinary in quality. The bread Tuscans use is salt-free (unsalted bread has been a Tuscan tradition since the 12th century — there are different explanations including a salt trade tax — which means it goes stale faster and is perfectly designed for bread-based cooking).

**Ribollita:** Winter bread soup — cannellini beans, cavolo nero (black kale), carrots, celery, stale bread, olive oil. Literally "re-boiled" (the next day's reheated leftover soup is considered better than the first day's). Not a summer dish — order it October–March.

**Crostini di fegatini:** Chicken liver pâté on toasted bread, typically with capers and anchovies. The most common Tuscan antipasto. Good anywhere; extraordinary when the liver is fresh.

**Peposo:** Beef slow-cooked in Chianti with extraordinary quantities of whole black peppercorns. The origin story: Brunelleschi's kilnmen — the tile workers who fired the bricks for the Dome's construction — made this dish in their kilns using the cheapest cuts of meat and the cheapest wine, slow-cooking it for hours in the residual heat. Brunelleschi ate it on the building site.

**Cantucci con vin santo:** The Tuscan dessert ritual — twice-baked almond biscuits dipped in vin santo (a sweet amber dessert wine made from dried Trebbiano and Malvasia grapes). Never dip cantucci in espresso. Never skip the dipping. Vin santo comes in a small glass; dip and wait for the biscotto to soften slightly before eating.

**Gelato:** Florence has dozens of excellent gelaterie. Signals of quality: natural colours (pistachio is pale grey-green, not electric green), stored in metal containers with lids rather than displayed in towering peaks, a short seasonal menu. **Gelateria dei Neri** (Via dei Neri, near Santa Croce) and **Sbrino** (Via dei Serragli, Oltrarno) are consistently mentioned by locals.

### Wine

**Chianti Classico DOCG:** The wine of the hills between Florence and Siena, made primarily from Sangiovese. The Gallo Nero (Black Rooster) seal on the bottle neck guarantees it's from the specific Chianti Classico zone. House Chianti at restaurants: €5–8 per quarter-litre. A bottle of good Chianti Classico: €25–40.

**Brunello di Montalcino:** Tuscany's most prestigious red, from the hills around Montalcino (90 minutes south). 100% Sangiovese Grosso, aged minimum 5 years before release. €60–150 per bottle at restaurants.

**Vernaccia di San Gimignano:** The white wine of San Gimignano (a day-trip destination from Florence). Dry, slightly mineral, perfect with lampredotto or white-fleshed fish.

**Wine windows (buchette del vino):** Small openings in the walls of Renaissance palazzi, used from the 16th century for selling wine directly from palazzo cellars to street buyers. They were revived during COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020 and many continue operating. Look for them in the Oltrarno — **Babae** on Via Santo Spirito has one.

### Where to Eat

**Trattoria Mario (Via Rosina 2):** Open since 1953. Communal tables, no reservations, daily changing menu, cash only, lunch only. The most honest value in Florence. Arrive at 12:00 when it opens. Budget €15–18 for a full lunch with wine.

**Buca dell'Orafo (Vicolo de' Girolami 28):** Near the Ponte Vecchio. Old-school Florentine — ribollita, bistecca, pasta with wild boar ragù. Dinner reservations needed.

**Trattoria Sostanza (Via del Porcellana 25, Oltrarno):** The oldest trattoria in Florence (opened 1869, still family-run). Famous for its burro pasta (pasta with butter, in a manner that is richer than it sounds) and its butter-fried artichokes.

**Fuori Porta (Via del Monte alle Croci 10, San Niccolò):** The best wine bar in Florence for an Oltrarno afternoon — hundreds of Tuscan and Italian labels, excellent crostini and cheese plates, an outdoor terrace.

## Day Trips from Florence

### Siena (1h15 by bus or car)

Medieval rival of Florence, with one of the most beautiful main squares in the world — Piazza del Campo, the shell-shaped brick square where the Palio horse race takes place twice yearly (2 July and 16 August — if these dates align with your visit, attend, because the Palio is one of Europe's most intense sporting and cultural events). The Duomo of Siena has striped marble interior and a floor of inlaid marble panels — extraordinary. Direct bus from Sita Nord station in Florence: approximately €8 return.

### Pisa (1h by train)

Primarily the Piazza dei Miracoli — the Cathedral, Baptistery, and Leaning Tower (currently 3.97 degrees off vertical after stabilisation work). Entry to the tower: €20, 300 steps, advance booking essential. The tower is better than you expect from the photos. The baptistery has extraordinary acoustics — ask a guide to demonstrate. Train: €9–13 return.

### Chianti Countryside

The wine-producing hills between Florence and Siena — Greve in Chianti, Panzano, Radda, Castellina. Best by car, though cycling is possible on the quieter roads. Wine tastings at Castello di Brolio (the estate that codified the Chianti Classico blend in the 19th century, spectacular castle, guided tastings from €15), Antinori nel Chianti Classico (the ultra-modern winery buried in a hillside, extraordinary architecture, tasting from €25).

### San Gimignano (1h20 by bus)

The medieval hill town of towers — in the Middle Ages, wealthy families competed by building towers (there were once 72; 14 survive, up to 54 metres). The interior of the Duomo has an extraordinary Last Judgement fresco cycle, and the town is small enough to cover on foot in 2–3 hours. Best visited early morning before the tour buses arrive at 10:00.

## Getting To and Around Florence

**Airports:** Florence Amerigo Vespucci (FLR), 5 km from the city — small but well-connected. Pisa International (PSA) — 80 minutes by train, a viable alternative with more Ryanair and Wizz Air connections. Bologna Guglielmo Marconi (BLQ) — 90 minutes by train, useful for broader flight options.

**From Bucharest:** Direct flights to Pisa with Wizz Air from €50 return, and to Bologna or Rome (with onward train). Florence itself has limited direct budget connections; the Pisa or Bologna combination works well.

**Train from Rome:** High-speed Frecciarossa Roma Termini → Firenze SMN: 1h30, €25–60 depending on booking lead time.

**In the city:** Walk everywhere. Florence's historic centre is 2 km across. ZTL (restricted traffic zone) covers almost all of the historic centre — cars without special permits (hotels can arrange this) cannot enter. Taxis are available but rarely necessary for in-city movement. Trams run to the train station and suburbs but don't serve the main tourist areas.

**Shoes:** The cobblestone streets of Florence — the irregular sampietrini that pave every piazza and alley — are hard on thin soles and can be genuinely slippery after rain. Proper walking shoes are not optional.

## Practical Tips

**Book the Dome first.** Before anything else. It sells out faster than the Uffizi.

**The Brunelleschi Pass requires a timed entry for the dome.** When you book, select your dome time slot — this is the anchor around which you schedule everything else. The other elements of the pass can be used flexibly within 72 hours.

**Coperto** (€2–3/person) is standard and legal at Florentine restaurants. Not a scam.

**Restaurant hours:** Most trattorias serve lunch 12:00–14:30 and dinner 19:30–22:00. Arriving at 19:30 means tourist competition; arriving at 20:30 means you eat alongside Florentines.

**Dress code for churches:** Covered shoulders and knees required at the Duomo, Santa Croce, Santa Maria Novella, and most churches. Shawls and cover-ups sold near entrances.

**Gelato tip:** Avoid any gelateria with fluorescent-coloured, towering peaks of gelato. These are tourist product, not real gelato. Natural colours, metal containers with lids, shorter menu.

**Water:** Tap water in Florence is safe and excellent (from the Apennine spring system). Ask for *acqua del rubinetto* (tap water) at restaurants. Free drinking fountains throughout the city.
