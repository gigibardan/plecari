You are looking at two options: London to New York direct, €900, 7 hours. Or London to New York via Reykjavik on Icelandair, €540, 10 hours. The difference is €360 and 3 extra hours. How do you decide?

The answer is not universal — it depends on the route, the actual saving, the hub quality, the ticket type, and your personal tolerance for travel complexity. This guide gives you the complete framework for any scenario.

## How much you actually save: European vs intercontinental routes

### Short European routes — layovers rarely make sense

On European destinations where the direct flight takes 2–4 hours, the price difference with a connection is typically minimal. You add risk and time without meaningful financial benefit.

| Route | Direct | With layover | Saving | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| London → Barcelona | €80–160 | €70–145 | €10–20 | **Not worth it** |
| London → Rome | €80–160 | €70–140 | €10–20 | **Not worth it** |
| London → Lisbon | €90–180 | €80–165 | €10–25 | **Rarely worth it** |
| London → Athens | €100–200 | €90–180 | €10–25 | **Rarely worth it** |
| Amsterdam → Istanbul | €80–160 | €70–145 | €10–20 | **Not worth it** |

**The conclusion for European routes:** saving €10–25 does not compensate for 2–3 extra hours of travel and the real risk of missing a connection. On short routes, direct almost always wins.

### Intercontinental routes — layovers can be decisive

On long distances, the price difference becomes substantial — and justifies extra hours.

## The decision formula: value per hour added

The practical tool experienced travellers use:

**Layover value = Saving (€) ÷ Hours added**

| Saving | Hours added | €/hour | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| €20 | 3 hours | €6.7/hour | ❌ Not worth it |
| €50 | 3 hours | €16.7/hour | ⚠️ Marginal |
| €150 | 4 hours | €37.5/hour | ✅ Worth it |
| €300 | 4 hours | €75/hour | ✅ Clearly worth it |
| €400 | 5 hours | €80/hour | ✅ No question |

**Personal threshold:** if the value of your leisure time during travel is below €40–50/hour, a layover saving €200+ is rational. If you are travelling for business or your time has high value, direct wins even with €200 price differences.

## The real risk: what happens when you miss a connection

### Scenario 1: Single booking (one PNR) — full protection

If both flights are on the same ticket (one booking reference), the airline is fully responsible for the connection:

**What you receive automatically:**
- Rebooking on the next available flight to your destination — at no extra cost
- Meals and drinks if the wait exceeds 2 hours
- Hotel accommodation and transfers if an overnight stay becomes necessary
- **EU261 compensation** if you arrive at your final destination 3+ hours late: **€250–600 per person**, depending on total route distance

**How to verify you have a single ticket:** your booking confirmation has one alphanumeric booking reference (e.g. ABC123). If you have two different codes for the two flights — you have separate tickets.

Full guide: [flight delay and cancellation rights EU261](/en/zboruri/flight-delay-passenger-rights).

### Scenario 2: Separate tickets — risk on you

If you booked London → Istanbul on one ticket and Istanbul → Bangkok on a separate booking (different PNR, possibly different airline), the first airline has zero obligation regarding the second flight:

**What happens if you miss the connection:**
- You must buy a new ticket at the current market price
- A last-minute intercontinental one-way: €500–1,500
- Hotel at the connection city: your expense
- Your return flight may be lost (typically non-refundable)

**When separate tickets can make sense:**
- The layover is very long (6+ hours) — near-zero risk of missing the connection
- The saving versus a single ticket is significant (€100+)
- You have travel insurance with explicit **missed connection** coverage
- You are travelling with hand luggage only — eliminates the baggage problem

## Baggage at layovers: exactly what happens

**Single ticket (one PNR):**
Bags are checked through to your final destination at the original check-in. At the connection point, you do nothing — ground handlers transfer them automatically. No customs, no re-check-in.

**Separate tickets — the full process:**
1. Collect bags at the connection airport arrivals belt
2. Clear customs and immigration (if an international connection)
3. Exit the arrivals hall with all bags
4. Find the check-in desk for the second airline
5. Re-check-in bags for the second flight
6. Clear security again

Time required: minimum 2–3 hours. With hand luggage only: 30–45 minutes.

**Practical recommendation:** if booking separate tickets on an intercontinental route, travel with hand luggage only. This reduces the transfer process to clearing security — manageable even in 90 minutes at efficient airports.

## Long layovers: turning transit time into a city visit

A 5–10 hour layover is not wasted time — it is a bonus destination if you manage it right.

### Istanbul — 5 to 8 hours

Metro M11 from Istanbul Airport: 38 minutes to Gayrettepe station. From there, M2 to Sultanahmet: 20 minutes. Total door-to-door from plane to Hagia Sophia: under 1 hour.

With 5 hours available: Sultanahmet Square, Hagia Sophia (free during prayer times, ticket required for tourist-hours access), the Grand Bazaar, a proper kebab lunch, and back to the airport with comfortable margin.

**Turkish Airlines Stopover Programme:** officially extends a connection into 1–3 nights in Istanbul with hotel packages at negotiated rates, included in the overall ticket. Register online at Turkish Airlines' stopover page.

### Amsterdam — 4 to 6 hours

Direct train Schiphol → Amsterdam Centraal: 15 minutes, €6. The canal rings of Jordaan and Leidseplein are 10 minutes' walk from Centraal. A warm stroopwafel from the Albert Cuyp Market, a coffee at a canal-side café, and the Rijksmuseum exterior — all feasible in 4 hours with time to spare.

### Vienna — 5 to 7 hours

S-Bahn S7 to Wien Hauptbahnhof: 16 minutes, €4.30. The Ringstrasse, Hofburg Palace exterior (free), Naschmarkt (open Monday–Saturday), and the Sachertorte at Café Sacher (€8.50). Vienna is the most elegant transit city in Europe — an hour here feels like it belongs in a different era.

### Dubai — 7 to 10 hours

Red Metro Line from Terminal 3: contactless card payment, no need to buy tickets. Dubai Mall, Burj Khalifa exterior (free, the observation deck requires booking), and JBR beach — all reachable within 30 minutes.

**Emirates Stopover Programme:** similar to Turkish Airlines' offering — hotel and tour packages at special rates for passengers with longer connections. Activatable online before travel.

### Reykjavik — 4 to 6 hours (Icelandair)

Icelandair's own stopover programme lets transatlantic passengers spend 1–7 nights in Iceland at no extra airfare cost — a genuine two-destination trip for the price of one transatlantic ticket.

## Open-jaw itineraries: the experienced traveller's tool

Open-jaw means flying into one city and returning from another:

**European examples:**
- Fly to Rome, return from Barcelona — see both cities without retracing
- Fly to Athens, return from Dubrovnik — the Adriatic and Aegean in one trip
- Fly to Lisbon, return from Porto — both cities naturally on one ticket

**Intercontinental examples:**
- Fly to Bangkok, return from Singapore — Southeast Asia without backtracking
- Fly to New York, return from Miami — East Coast + Florida on one booking
- Fly to Tokyo, return from Osaka — Japan from north to south

**Cost:** typically similar to a standard return ticket on a single route — sometimes marginally more expensive, sometimes the same. The experience is significantly richer.

**Search engines that find open-jaw well:** Kiwi.com (specialist in non-standard routing combinations), Google Flights with the Explore function, Skyscanner with "Everywhere" as destination.

## Virtual interlining: Kiwi.com's specific advantage

Traditional booking systems only combine flights from airlines with interline agreements. Kiwi.com's virtual interlining combines flights from any two airlines — including low-cost carriers that have no agreement with each other — onto what functions as a single itinerary.

**What Kiwi Guarantee adds:** if you miss a connection on a virtual interline combination (a flight operated by two airlines with no interline agreement), Kiwi will rebook you or refund the unused leg. This extends some of the protection of a single-ticket booking to combinations that would otherwise be separate tickets.

**When virtual interlining is useful:** when a combination of two low-cost carriers (Ryanair + Wizz Air, or Ryanair + easyJet) produces a significantly lower price than any single-airline routing — and you want protection beyond raw separate tickets.
