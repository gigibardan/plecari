A flight delayed 3 hours on a route like London to Barcelona entitles you to 400 EUR — per passenger. A family of 4 gets 1,600 EUR. The tickets might have cost 200 EUR total. The compensation is 8 times the ticket price.

Most passengers don't know this. Fewer still claim the money. EU Regulation 261/2004 has been in force since 2004 and applies automatically — it doesn't depend on the airline, the ticket price, or the class of travel. It's a right, not a favor. And yet, millions of European passengers lose hundreds of millions of euros every year that they could legally claim.

This guide explains everything: when you're entitled, exactly how much you get, how to claim step by step, what to do when the airline refuses, when you're NOT entitled, what changes with the 2026 reform, and how to maximize your chances of success.

## EU Regulation 261/2004 — what the law says

### The 3 situations where you get compensation

**1. Flight delayed 3+ hours at the final destination**
It doesn't matter how long the delay was at departure — what matters is the time you arrive at your final destination. If the flight departs 2 hours late but lands only 2 hours 50 minutes late, you do NOT qualify. If it departs 1 hour late but due to conditions arrives 3 hours 10 minutes late, you qualify.

Important: arrival time is calculated from the moment the aircraft doors open at the gate, NOT from the moment of touchdown on the runway. The difference can be 10-20 minutes at major airports.

**2. Flight cancelled with less than 14 days' notice**
If the airline cancels the flight and informs you less than 14 days before departure, you're entitled to compensation. Exception: if the airline offers an alternative flight arriving at a time close to the original (±1-2 hours, depending on how early they informed you).

Exact rules for cancellation:
- **14+ days before:** no compensation (only rerouting or refund)
- **7-13 days before:** compensation if the alternative departs 2+ hours earlier OR arrives 4+ hours later
- **Under 7 days before:** compensation if the alternative departs 1+ hour earlier OR arrives 2+ hours later

**3. Denied boarding (overbooking)**
The airline sold more tickets than seats on the aircraft, and you're the one who doesn't fit. Compensation is automatic, with no extraordinary circumstances exception. This is the only case where the airline cannot make any excuses.

**Important:** airlines will ask for "volunteers" to give up their seat, usually offering vouchers or upgrades. You're not obligated to accept — but if you accept voluntarily, you waive your right to compensation. Accept only if the offer is better than the legal compensation.

### The amount does NOT depend on the ticket price

This is the point most travelers miss. A €19.99 Ryanair ticket on a London-Milan route delayed 3 hours qualifies you for €250 — 12.5 times the ticket price. A €45 easyJet ticket on London-Barcelona qualifies for €400 — almost 9 times the price.

Low-cost airlines have no legal excuse to offer less. EU 261 applies identically to all carriers: Ryanair, easyJet, Wizz Air, Lufthansa, British Airways, Air France.

## Where EU 261 applies

### Flights covered

| Departure | Arrival | Airline | Covered? |
|---|---|---|---|
| EU airport | Anywhere | Any airline | **YES** |
| Non-EU airport | EU airport | EU airline (Lufthansa, Ryanair, etc.) | **YES** |
| Non-EU airport | EU airport | Non-EU airline (Emirates, Delta, etc.) | **NO** |
| Non-EU airport | Non-EU airport | Any | **NO** |

**Simple rule:** if the flight departs from the EU, you're covered regardless of airline. If it arrives in the EU but doesn't depart from the EU, you're covered only if the airline is European.

### Countries included (beyond the EU)

EU 261 also applies in:
- **Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein** (European Economic Area)
- **Switzerland** (bilateral agreement)
- **United Kingdom** — UK 261, a nearly identical rule post-Brexit
- **French overseas territories** (Guadeloupe, Réunion, etc.)

### Concrete examples — covered or not?

| Flight | Covered? | Why |
|---|---|---|

| Turkish Airlines Istanbul → London, 3h delay | **NO** | Non-EU airline, non-EU departure |
| Lufthansa Frankfurt → New York, 5h delay | **YES** | EU departure |
| Delta New York → Paris, 4h delay | **NO** | Non-EU airline, non-EU departure |
| Ryanair London → Barcelona, cancelled | **YES** | UK departure (UK 261) + EU airline |
| Emirates Dubai → London, cancelled | **NO** | Non-EU airline, non-EU departure |
| Wizz Air Abu Dhabi → Vienna, 4h delay | **YES** | EU airline, arriving in EU |

## Connecting flights — special rules

### When you qualify on connections

If you have a connecting ticket (flight A → B → C) purchased as a single booking, the delay is calculated at **the final destination** (C), not at the intermediate stop (B).

**Example:** flight London → Frankfurt → New York. The London-Frankfurt leg is delayed 1h30, you miss the connection, and arrive in New York 5 hours late. Compensation: **€600** (total distance London-New York is over 3,500 km).

**Key rule:** if both segments are on the same booking (one PNR/booking reference), the rule applies. If you bought the tickets separately (different bookings), each segment is evaluated individually — and you lose the right on the second segment if you arrived at the connection on time but missed the flight for your own reasons.

### Connection with different airlines

If the ticket is on a single booking but segments are operated by different airlines (codeshare), the responsible airline is the one that actually operated the segment that caused the delay.

### Missed connection due to security queues

If you arrived at the airport on time but missed your flight due to security queues, the responsibility lies with the airport, not the airline. EU 261 doesn't apply — but you can file a complaint with the airport.

## Extraordinary circumstances — when the airline does NOT pay

### What counts as extraordinary circumstances (airline doesn't have to pay)

| Circumstance | Details |
|---|---|
| Severe weather | Major storms, blizzards, volcanic ash, hurricanes — conditions making flying unsafe |
| External strikes | Air traffic controller strikes, airport staff strikes, border police strikes |
| Security risks | Bomb threats, terrorist alerts, airspace closures |
| Natural disasters | Earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, floods affecting the airport |
| Political instability | War situations, civil unrest near the airport |
| Unruly passengers | Flight diverted/delayed due to a disruptive passenger |
| Bird strikes | If causing significant damage requiring inspection/repairs |

### What does NOT count as extraordinary circumstances (airline MUST pay)

This is where airlines lose in court, and where informed passengers win:

| Situation | Why it's NOT extraordinary | Reference ruling |
|---|---|---|
| Aircraft technical problems | Part of normal operations; airline must maintain the fleet | CJEU Wallentin-Hermann (C-549/07) |
| Engine defects found at check | Maintenance is the airline's responsibility | CJEU van der Lans (C-257/14) |
| Pilot/crew strikes (airline's own) | The airline has control over its labor relations | CJEU TUIfly (C-195/17) |
| Crew shortages (illness) | Airlines must maintain standby crew | Multiple national rulings |
| Bad weather at a previous airport | The delay isn't related to your direct flight | Restrictive CJEU interpretation |
| Bird strike WITHOUT significant damage | If inspection confirms no damage, the flight must operate | CJEU Pešková (C-315/15) |

### The trick airlines use

Statistically, airlines succeed in contesting only **47% of claims** (2024 data). This means over half of their challenges fail. The most common strategy: invoke "technical issues" and hope the passenger gives up. The Lufthansa Group is considered the most aggressive in contesting claims on technical grounds.

**Tip:** if the airline invokes extraordinary circumstances, ask them for the specific reason in writing. Then verify whether it actually qualifies — or use AirHelp/Compensair who know exactly what holds up in court.

## Beyond compensation — your care rights

Even when you do NOT have the right to financial compensation (extraordinary circumstances), you ALWAYS have the right to care. The airline must provide:

### Right to Care

| Delay duration | What you must receive FREE |
|---|---|
| 2+ hours (under 1,500 km) or 3+ hours (1,500-3,500 km) or 4+ hours (over 3,500 km) | Food and drinks, 2 phone calls or emails |
| Over 5 hours | Right to abandon the journey + full ticket refund |
| Overnight | Hotel + airport-hotel transport (round trip) |

### What to do if the airline doesn't provide care

1. **Keep all receipts** — meals, drinks, hotel, taxi
2. Spend reasonably (not a 5-star hotel for an economy flight)
3. Request reimbursement after the trip, with receipts as proof
4. If they refuse, include the costs in your compensation claim

### Right to refund or rerouting

When the flight is cancelled, you choose:
- **Full refund** of the ticket (within 7 days, per the regulation)
- **Rerouting** on another flight, at the earliest available date, even on a different airline
- **Rerouting at a later date** that suits you (if the airline offers)

**Important:** the airline must reroute you on another carrier if they don't have an available flight. Many airlines "forget" to mention this option — ask for it explicitly.

### Downgrade rights

If you're involuntarily moved to a lower class (e.g., from Business to Economy), you're entitled to a fare refund:
- **30%** of the ticket price for flights under 1,500 km
- **50%** of the ticket price for flights 1,500-3,500 km
- **75%** of the ticket price for flights over 3,500 km

## What's changing — the EU 261 reform of 2026

The European Parliament approved the reform in July 2026. Implementation is estimated for the second half of 2027. What changes:

### Changes for passengers

| Aspect | Current (2026) | After reform (~2027) |
|---|---|---|
| Claim deadline | Varies by country (1-10 years) | **9 months uniform** EU-wide |
| Airline must respond within | Not legally defined | **30 days** |
| Claim instructions | Not mandatory | Airline must send instructions within **4 days** of incident |
| Seats for children under 14 | Extra charge at some airlines | **Free** — child sits next to adult |
| Seats for disabled passengers | Variable | **Free** next to companion |
| Carry-on bag | Low-cost airlines charge extra | **Included in the displayed price** |
| Small personal item | Variable | **Guaranteed free** (backpack/small bag) |
| Name correction on ticket | €20-50 fee at some airlines | **Free** |
| Printed boarding pass | Fee at some airlines | **Free** |
| Return flight if you miss outbound | Some airlines cancel the return | **Protected** — return stays valid |
| Rerouting | On the airline's own flights | If no flight within **3 hours**, must use another carrier |

### What stays the same

- Compensation amounts: €250/400/600 (unchanged)
- 3-hour delay threshold (unchanged)
- General principle: airline pays unless extraordinary circumstances

### Hotel limited to 3 nights

Controversial addition: in cases of extraordinary circumstances, the airline's obligation to provide a hotel is limited to **3 nights**. After 3 nights, you're on your own. For normal situations (technical problems, internal strikes), there's no limit.

## Voucher or cash? What to accept

### The basic rule: you're entitled to CASH

Under EU 261, compensation is paid in money — cash, bank transfer, or cheque. Airlines prefer offering vouchers (flight credit, service vouchers) because it's cheaper for them. You're not obligated to accept vouchers.

### When it's worth accepting a voucher

- If the airline offers a voucher of **significantly higher value** than the compensation (e.g., €500 voucher vs €250 compensation) AND you're certain you'll fly with them within the validity period
- If you urgently need an alternative flight and the airline offers an upgrade + additional voucher

### When to refuse a voucher

- If the voucher value equals or is less than the legal compensation
- If the voucher has a short validity period
- If you're not sure you'll fly with that airline
- If you need money, not flight credit

## Questions people Google

**Do I get compensation if I bought the ticket through an agency?**
Yes. EU 261 applies to passengers, not to ticket purchasers. It doesn't matter where you bought the ticket — from the airline, Skyscanner, Kiwi, or a travel agency. You file the claim with the airline, not the agency.

**Do children get compensation?**
Yes, each passenger with their own ticket qualifies. Children with a ticket (even infants with a reduced fare on a parent's lap) qualify. Children under 2 WITHOUT a ticket (€0 fare) do not qualify.

**Does it matter if I was late to the airport?**
Yes — if you arrived late to the gate and missed the flight, you do NOT have the right to compensation. EU 261 applies only when the airline causes the delay/cancellation. But if you missed a connection due to the delay of the first flight (on the same booking), you qualify.

**Do I get compensation for charter flights?**
Yes, EU 261 applies to charter flights departing from the EU as well. Including charter flights in package holidays.

**Can I claim compensation for a flight from 2 years ago?**
Yes, if the claim deadline hasn't passed (varies by country: 3 years in Germany/Romania, 5 years in France/Spain, 6 years in the UK/Ireland). Many passengers successfully claim compensation for flights 1-2 years old.

**The airline offered me an upgrade to Business. Do I still get compensation?**
Yes! The upgrade is a bonus, not a replacement for compensation. You can have both the upgrade and the compensation — they're separate things.

**If I received a ticket refund, do I also get compensation?**
Yes. The ticket refund and EU 261 compensation are separate rights. The refund is your money back for the service not received. The compensation is for the inconvenience.

**Does EU 261 work if I don't live in the EU?**
Yes. EU 261 applies based on the flight route, not the passenger's citizenship or residence. An American citizen on a flight from Frankfurt to New York has the same rights as a European citizen.
