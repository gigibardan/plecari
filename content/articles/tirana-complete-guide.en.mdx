There is a moment when any tourist arriving in Tirana for the first time realises their expectations were completely wrong. They didn't know what to expect — Albania was so isolated for decades that almost no mental images exist in advance. And that's precisely what makes it fascinating.

Tirana is an urban transformation experiment without precedent in Europe. <cite index="460-1">Albania's capital has gone from a modest town to a metropolis of nearly a million people, with vibrant colours and a thriving nightlife scene.</cite> Enver Hoxha — the only communist dictator to break relations with both the USSR and China — left behind 750,000 concrete bunkers throughout the country (more than any other country in the world) and a city of grey Stalinist architecture. Today the blocks are painted in primary colours, the bunkers are museums and the dictator's pyramid has been turned into a playground for children.

<cite index="451-1">Tirana is one of Europe's most compact and most walkable capitals — the vast majority of attractions are within 2 km of Skanderbeg Square.</cite>

## Tirana at a Glance — Essential Facts

**Country:** Albania — not in the EU (official candidate since 2014), not in Schengen.

**Population:** approximately 900,000 — continuously growing.

**Currency:** Albanian Lek (ALL). <cite index="465-1">1 EUR ≈ 100 ALL in 2026 — an extremely simple calculation: divide the ALL amount by 100 to get the EUR price.</cite>

**Language:** Albanian. English is well spoken in the tourist area and by younger generations. Italian is widely understood (historical influence and diaspora in Italy).

**Visa:** EU and Romanian citizens enter Albania without a visa — valid ID card or passport, max 90 days.

**Direct flights from Bucharest:** Wizz Air, OTP→TIA, ~1h45.

**Average budget:** <cite index="461-1">30–50 EUR/day (mid-range) — 40–60% cheaper than Greece or Croatia for comparable experiences.</cite>

## Albanian Lek — Quick Guide

1 EUR = 100 ALL. 1,000 ALL = 10 EUR. 5,000 ALL = 50 EUR.

**Critical ATM warning:** <cite index="463-1">Albanian ATMs charge a fixed fee of 700–800 ALL (~7–8 EUR) per withdrawal, regardless of amount.</cite> Withdraw the maximum permitted amount in one go to minimise fees.

**Exchange offices** (Kembim Valutor) in Tirana's centre offer rates far better than the airport. Don't exchange at the airport if you can avoid it.

**Cash is king:** <cite index="463-1">Albania runs on cash — public transport, small restaurants, taxis and markets accept cash only.</cite> Always keep 3,000–5,000 ALL (30–50 EUR) in your pocket.

## What to See in Tirana

### Skanderbeg Square (Sheshi Skënderbej) — The Symbolic Centre

<cite index="451-1">Skanderbeg Square is Tirana's vast central square, surrounded by the National History Museum, Et'hem Bey Mosque, the Clock Tower and government buildings — a living timeline of the city.</cite>

**Skanderbeg Statue** — the 11-metre bronze equestrian statue of national hero Gjergj Kastrioti Skënderbej, the leader of Albanian resistance against the Ottomans in the 15th century. <cite index="451-1">The square is free, accessible 24/7. Most beautiful moment: at sunset when the marble catches the golden light.</cite>

**Et'hem Bey Mosque** — the 18th-century mosque with exceptional interior frescoes, one of the most beautiful in the Balkans. <cite index="458-1">Free entry; hours 9:00–17:00 (closed at prayer times).</cite>

**Clock Tower** — <cite index="458-1">200 ALL (~2 EUR), 90 steps, panoramic view.</cite>

**National History Museum** — Albania's largest museum, covering history from ancient Illyria to communism. <cite index="451-1">700 ALL (~7 EUR); Tuesday–Saturday 10:00–17:00, Sunday 9:00–14:00, closed Mondays.</cite>

### Bunk'Art 2 — The Secret Police Bunker

<cite index="451-1">Bunk'Art 2 is right in the city centre, 5 minutes from Skanderbeg Square, and is focused on the communist Ministry of Internal Affairs and the Sigurimi (Albanian secret police).</cite>

<cite index="454-1">Entry: 500 ALL (~5 EUR). Hours: daily 9:00–19:00, closed Mondays. Allow 45–60 minutes.</cite>

The bunker was built in secret under an apartment block — residents above had no idea what was beneath them. The exhibition documents the Hoxha regime's methods of surveillance, torture and repression — one of the most chilling and best-documented museum experiences in Eastern Europe.

### Bunk'Art 1 — Hoxha's Nuclear Bunker

<cite index="451-1">Bunk'Art 1 is a huge nuclear bunker carved into Mount Dajti, built in 1978 for the communist elite — with 100+ rooms including apartments, a conference hall and nuclear survival facilities.</cite>

**Getting there:** Dajti Express cable car (1,000–1,200 ALL, ~10–12 EUR, return) or taxi. <cite index="451-1">Bunk'Art 1 entry: 700–800 ALL (~7–8 EUR).</cite>

### The Pyramid of Enver Hoxha — Radical Transformation

<cite index="456-1">The Pyramid was built in 1988 as a mausoleum for dictator Enver Hoxha.</cite> It stood abandoned and vandalised for decades, the most visible symbol of Albania's communist past.

In 2022, the Pyramid was renovated and transformed into a **cultural centre and youth space** — with slides on the exterior sides for children, a belvedere terrace at the top and interactive exhibition spaces. Entry is free.

### Blloku District — Former Forbidden Zone, Now the Trendiest Neighbourhood

<cite index="451-1">Blloku was for decades a completely forbidden zone for ordinary citizens — where the communist elite lived, with Enver Hoxha at its centre.</cite> Today it is Tirana's most vibrant and cosmopolitan neighbourhood, with specialty coffee cafés, concept restaurants, bars and boutiques.

The contrast with the past is palpable — you can sit for a coffee on a restaurant terrace where 35 years ago ordinary citizens were not permitted to set foot.

### Pazari i Ri — The New Covered Market

<cite index="456-1">Pazari i Ri is Tirana's central market under a modern glass and steel hall, with stalls of fruit, vegetables, honey, olive oil and local products.</cite> Perfect for tasting Albanian produce and an authentic walk away from the tourist zone. Free.

### Xhiro — The Albanian Evening Promenade

Xhiro (pronounced "dziro") is one of the most beautiful traditions of Albanian urban life — a collective evening walk along the main boulevard, practised by families together, from grandparents to grandchildren.

<cite index="453-1">Every evening at sunset, Skanderbeg Square and Dëshmorët e Kombit Boulevard fill with Tiranese on xhiro — families, couples, groups of young people, all walking at the same slow, social pace.</cite>

It is not a scheduled tourist attraction — it is simply the life of the city. Join the rhythm.

## Food and Drink in Tirana

### What You Must Eat

**Byrek** — Albanian pastry with cheese, meat or spinach, thin filo dough. Essential street food. At a local bakery: 100–200 ALL (1–2 EUR). The classic Albanian breakfast.

**Tavë kosi** — lamb baked in yoghurt with egg and butter — the national speciality, creamy and rich. 500–800 ALL (5–8 EUR) at a traditional restaurant.

**Fërgese Tiranase** — a gratin of peppers, tomatoes and local cheese, Tirana's emblematic appetiser. 300–500 ALL.

**Trilece** — the milk cake with three textures and caramel — Albania's national dessert (of Turkish-Balkan origin). 200–300 ALL (2–3 EUR).

**Kafe turke** — Turkish coffee served in a small pot with grounds at the bottom. 100–150 ALL (1–1.50 EUR). Albania has an intense coffee culture — cafés are full at any hour.

### Rakia — The Albanian Spirit

Rakia (local fruit brandy) is Albania's traditional drink — distilled from plums, cherries or grapes. A shot at a restaurant: 100–200 ALL (1–2 EUR). This is the drink with which Albanians welcome guests.

### Where to Eat

**Budget (300–700 ALL/meal):**
- Byrek bakeries in the centre — the perfect breakfast under 2 EUR
- Restaurants around Pazari i Ri

**Mid-range (700–1,500 ALL/person):**
- Restaurants in **Blloku** — Tirana's best gastronomy scene
- **Mullixhiu** — contemporary Albanian restaurant with modern interpretations of traditional cuisine (reservation recommended)

**Caution:** <cite index="461-1">restaurants directly on Skanderbeg Square charge 30–50% more than those on adjacent streets.</cite>

## Practical Tips

**ATM** — withdraw the maximum amount in one go (fixed fee ~7 EUR/transaction). Don't use Euronet. Kembim Valutor exchange offices in the centre have far better rates than the airport.

**Bolt** — works in Tirana, far more reliable and cheaper than street taxis.

**Albania is not in Schengen** — no visa needed as an EU/Romanian citizen, but passport/ID card is mandatory.

**Albanian hospitality (Besa)** — Albanians have a deeply cultural code of hospitality. If invited to a meal or coffee by an Albanian, accept — refusal can be considered offensive.
