Turkey generates more travel anxiety searches than almost any other popular destination. The question "Is Turkey safe?" appears thousands of times a day from prospective visitors, driven by a combination of regional news, social media amplification, and genuine uncertainty. This guide gives you the factual answer based on current government advisories and the practical reality on the ground.

## What the travel advisories actually say

Before anything else: the authoritative sources. Every government with a significant number of citizens travelling to Turkey publishes regularly updated travel advice. Here is what they say as of July 2026:

**UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO):**
- Advises against all travel to areas within 10 km of the Syrian border (Hatay province) and to areas of southeastern Turkey near the Iraqi border
- Does NOT advise against travel to Antalya, Istanbul, Izmir, Bodrum, Marmaris, Fethiye, or other main tourist areas
- Standard precautions recommended for terrorism risk in major cities

**US State Department:**
- Level 2 (Exercise Increased Caution) for Turkey as a whole
- "Do Not Travel" for specific southeastern provinces near Syrian, Iraqi, and Iranian borders
- No specific warning for coastal tourist areas

**EU member states:**
- Similar split assessment: avoid southeast, normal precautions for tourist areas

**The key point:** no major Western government advises against travel to Turkey's tourist destinations. The risk alerts are geographically specific and clearly separated from the Mediterranean coast and Istanbul.

## The geography that matters

Turkey is a large country — 784,000 km², comparable to the combined area of France and Germany. The southeastern provinces where conflict risk exists are separated from the tourist coast by enormous distances:

| Tourist destination | Distance to Syrian border | Distance to Iraqi border |
|---|---|---|
| Antalya | ~1,200 km | ~1,500 km |
| Istanbul | ~1,700 km | ~2,000 km |
| Bodrum | ~1,400 km | ~1,700 km |
| Marmaris | ~1,350 km | ~1,650 km |
| Cappadocia | ~900 km | ~1,100 km |

For context: 1,200 km is the distance from London to Warsaw, or from Rome to Copenhagen. Events in southeastern Turkey are physically disconnected from where tourists spend their time in the same way events in one part of a large continent are disconnected from another.

## Tourist areas: current situation

### Antalya and the Turkish Riviera

Antalya is Turkey's most popular tourist destination and the anchor of the Turkish Riviera — the Mediterranean coastal strip that includes Kemer, Side, Alanya, and Belek. The area hosts millions of European tourists annually, with large resort hotels operated by international chains (Rixos, Titanic, Delphin, Club Med, and others).

**Current situation:** fully operational. International charter flights from across Europe continue to serve Antalya Airport (AYT), which handles over 35 million passengers per year. No travel advisory targets this region specifically.

**The resort area reality:** Antalya's major hotels are effectively self-contained holiday environments — all-inclusive properties with private beaches, multiple pools, entertainment programmes, and on-site facilities. For many visitors, the geopolitical context of the broader region becomes abstract when the day consists of moving between the beach and the buffet.

### Istanbul

Istanbul is Turkey's largest city, its cultural capital, and one of the world's great urban destinations. The city of 16 million people spans two continents across the Bosphorus Strait.

**Key attractions operating normally:** Hagia Sophia (now a mosque — dress modestly for entry, non-Muslims welcome), Topkapi Palace, the Blue Mosque (Sultan Ahmed Mosque), the Grand Bazaar, the Spice Market, the Bosphorus cruise, Galata Tower, and the Dolmabahçe Palace.

**Safety in practice:** Istanbul is a safe city by international standards for most of its urban area. The tourist districts (Sultanahmet, Beyoğlu, Karaköy) are heavily visited and policed. Standard urban precautions apply: watch for pickpockets in crowded areas, use Uber/Bolt rather than unlicensed taxis, and be alert to tourist-specific scams (see below).

### Bodrum, Marmaris, Fethiye — the Aegean coast

Turkey's Aegean coast attracts a different type of visitor — more sailing, more upmarket, and geographically opposite to the risk areas. Bodrum is the centre of Turkey's luxury tourism scene; Marmaris is a large resort town popular with British tourists; Fethiye and the surrounding area (Ölüdeniz, Kayaköy, the Turquoise Coast) offer some of the most beautiful scenery in the Mediterranean.

All operate normally with no specific travel advisory concerns.

### eSIM: buy before you land (critical)

Turkey is not in the EU. Your EU free roaming allowance does not apply. Without a local data solution, you will be charged by your home carrier at non-EU roaming rates — typically €10–15 per gigabyte. A week of moderate data use could cost €50–100 in roaming charges.

### Travel insurance: mandatory, not optional

Turkey is outside the EU. The European Health Insurance Card (EHIC) is completely invalid — Turkey has no bilateral healthcare agreement with EU member states. Without private travel insurance, you have no medical coverage at all.

**The cost of treatment without insurance:**
- Emergency doctor visit at a private clinic: €100–300
- Night in a private hospital: €500–2,000
- Surgery: €3,000–15,000
- Medical evacuation by air to home country: €15,000–40,000

Turkish private hospitals in tourist areas are generally good — modern facilities, English-speaking staff, and prompt care. The quality exists; the cost without insurance is the risk.

**Travel insurance for Turkey costs €15–25 for a week.** The math does not require explanation.

**What to verify in your policy:**
- Minimum €30,000 medical cover (standard for Schengen visa; appropriate for Turkey too)
- Medical evacuation explicitly covered
- 24/7 emergency assistance line
- Coverage for water sports and activities if relevant to your trip

### e-Visa: apply before departure

Most Western nationalities require an e-Visa for Turkey, obtained at evisa.gov.tr.

**Who needs it:** UK citizens, US citizens, Australian and New Zealand citizens, Canadians, and most non-EU nationalities. Some EU citizens can enter without a visa — check your specific nationality at the Turkish Ministry of Foreign Affairs website.

**The process:** apply at evisa.gov.tr, pay by card (approximately $50–60 depending on nationality), receive approval by email (typically within minutes to a few hours). Print or save the e-Visa on your phone — you need to show it at passport control along with your passport.

**Validity:** typically 180 days from issue, maximum 90-day stay. Multiple entry in most cases.

**Apply before you travel** — doing it at the airport or upon arrival is possible at some airports but slower and occasionally problematic.

### Passport, not ID card

Turkey requires a valid passport from all visitors — national ID cards are not accepted. Your passport must be valid for at least 6 months beyond your entry date. If your passport expires in less than 6 months from when you plan to enter Turkey, renew it before travelling.

## Turkey-specific scams to know

Istanbul in particular has well-documented tourist scams that have operated for decades. Knowing them makes them easy to avoid.

**The friendly bar invitation:** a local (or someone posing as one) strikes up a genuinely pleasant conversation, invites you for drinks at "his friend's bar," and escorts you somewhere. The bill arrives at €150–400 for drinks you may not have ordered. The establishment will have enforcers to ensure payment. Solution: politely decline any unsolicited invitation to go somewhere specific.

**The shoe shine "drop":** a shoe shiner apparently accidentally drops his brush in front of you. When you pick it up and return it, he insists on shining your shoes as a thank you. The "service" costs €10–20. Solution: do not pick up the brush.

**Unlicensed taxis:** taxis that approach you at tourist sites or airports often charge 3–5x the legitimate rate. Use Uber or Bolt — available in Istanbul, Ankara, Izmir, and Antalya. Fare shown before the trip, GPS-tracked, card payment. Eliminates all taxi fraud.

**Currency exchange by hand:** anyone offering to change money on the street. Never. Always use bank ATMs (not standalone machines on tourist streets) or official exchange bureaux inside commercial premises.

**Grand Bazaar pricing:** this is not a scam — it is the culture. Prices in the Grand Bazaar are starting points for negotiation. Accepting the first price offered means significantly overpaying. Starting at 50% of the stated price and meeting around 60–70% is standard practice. This applies to bazaars throughout Turkey.

## What to eat and drink in Turkey

Turkish cuisine is one of the world's great food traditions, and eating well in Turkey is inexpensive by European standards.

**Must-try:**
- **Kahvaltı (Turkish breakfast):** an extraordinary spread of small dishes — cheeses, olives, honey, clotted cream, tomatoes, cucumbers, eggs, bread, and unlimited tea. €4–8/person at a local breakfast café. The most generous breakfast tradition in Europe.
- **Kebab (the real version):** Adana kebab (spiced minced lamb on a flat skewer), İskender (döner over pide with tomato sauce and butter), Şiş (cubed lamb or chicken). €5–10 at a local lokanta.
- **Meze:** small plates served with rakı (anise spirit) or beer — hummus, eggplant dishes, stuffed grape leaves, fresh cheese, böreks. The full meze experience at a good meyhane (Turkish tavern): €15–25/person with drinks.
- **Pide:** Turkish flatbread baked with toppings in a wood-fired oven. A boat-shaped pide with cheese and egg costs €4–6.
- **Turkish tea (çay):** served in small tulip-shaped glasses everywhere, always, and always free in shops where you are browsing.

**Tap water:** do not drink tap water in Turkey. Bottled water is cheap (€0.50 for a large bottle) and universally available.

**Alcohol:** Turkey is a Muslim-majority country but alcohol is widely available in tourist areas, restaurants, and supermarkets. Avoid drinking visibly in non-tourist areas and near mosques.
