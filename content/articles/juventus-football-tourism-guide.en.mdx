# Juventus Football Tourism Guide: The Old Lady in Black and White

![Allianz Stadium Turin exterior](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1551958219-acbc595d9b1e?w=1200&q=80)

*Allianz Stadium — Italy's most modern football venue, home of the Old Lady*

**36 Serie A titles.** The most in Italian football history — almost double the total of the nearest rivals. **2 Champions League trophies, 4 European finals** in the past three decades. A purpose-built stadium that was the first in Italy to be owned outright by a football club. And a shirt — black and white stripes, immediately recognisable on every continent — that has been worn by Platini, Zidane, Buffon, Del Piero and Ronaldo.

Juventus is *La Vecchia Signora* — The Old Lady — and whatever you feel about them as a rival supporter, as a football tourist in Italy you cannot ignore what they represent. Turin — the industrial northwest city that built Fiats, invented vermouth and produced the most successful club in Italian football history — is waiting for you.

## Juventus: Club History

### Foundation — 1897

**Juventus Football Club** was founded on **1 November 1897** by a group of secondary school students in Turin — among the youngest founding groups in European football history. The name *Juventus* is Latin for *youth* — an appropriately ambitious choice.

The original colours were pink and black. In **1903**, following a request from British expat John Savage, Notts County (the oldest professional football club in the world) donated strips in black and white. The colours have never changed. The symbolism: white for purity, black for strength.

### The Agnelli Era Begins — 1923

The purchase of Juventus by the **Agnelli family** (owners of FIAT) in 1923 transformed everything. Financial stability, corporate ambition, and a deep connection to Turin's industrial identity arrived simultaneously. The first Scudetto under Agnelli ownership came in 1926.

### The Golden Quintet — Five Consecutive Titles (1930–1935)

Five consecutive Serie A championships — a performance that would not be equalled until Juventus themselves later surpassed it. The players of this era formed the core of **Italy's 1934 World Cup-winning squad** — ten Juventus players in the final squad. Turin was the unofficial capital of world football.

### The European Pioneer Era — Platini and the Champions Cup (1982–1986)

**Michel Platini** — three consecutive Ballon d'Or awards (1983, 1984, 1985), the greatest European player of the decade — signed for Juventus in 1982 and ignited the most glorious European era in the club's history to that point.

Under coach Giovanni Trapattoni, Juventus won the **1985 European Cup** in Brussels against Liverpool — a match shadowed by the Heysel Stadium disaster, where 39 fans died in a stadium crush before kick-off. The trophy came with a shadow that has never fully disappeared from European football's collective memory.

**European trophy haul in this period:** 1977 UEFA Cup, 1984 Cup Winners' Cup, 1984 UEFA Super Cup, **1985 European Cup** — the only club outside England and Spain to have won all three major European trophies.

### Baggio, Zidane, and the Second Champions League (1992–1998)

**Roberto Baggio** — *Il Divin Codino* (The Divine Ponytail), Italy's most beloved player of his generation — won the **1993 Ballon d'Or** wearing the bianconero shirt. His penalty miss in the 1994 World Cup final remains one of football's defining images.

**Zinedine Zidane** — arguably the most elegant player in football history — spent five years at Juventus (1996–2001), winning two Scudetti before Real Madrid paid the then-world record transfer fee to take him to Spain.

**1996 Champions League final:** 4–2 against Ajax (Van der Sar, Kluivert, De Boer). The second European Cup in the club's history.

### Calciopoli and Relegation — 2006

**2006** remains the darkest chapter in modern Juventus history. The *Calciopoli* scandal — revealing inappropriate contacts between club officials and match officials — led to relegation to Serie B and the stripping of two Serie A titles (2004–05 and 2005–06).

The club's response revealed character that transcends sporting results. Captain **Alessandro Del Piero** rejected offers from wealthier clubs to stay in the second division with Juventus. The 2006–07 Serie B season ended with a record-breaking promotion to Serie A, and the club's next decade was the most dominant in Italian football history.

### The Nine-Peat — Scudetti 2012–2020

Under **Antonio Conte** (2011–14) and **Massimiliano Allegri** (2014–19), Juventus constructed the longest domestic dominance streak in any major European league of the past generation: **nine consecutive Serie A titles**. The record: unbroken.

The squad during this era: **Gianluigi Buffon** (considered by many the greatest goalkeeper in football history), Andrea Pirlo (returning to Italy from Milan), Carlos Tevez, Arturo Vidal, Paul Pogba, and — from 2018 — **Cristiano Ronaldo**, who chose Turin at 33 specifically to win the Champions League that had eluded him in Italy.

**The Champions League remained elusive:** Two finals lost (2015 vs. Barcelona 1–3, 2017 vs. Real Madrid 1–4) are the pain of the era. Ronaldo's three seasons (101 goals in 134 appearances) produced spectacular football without the ultimate European prize.

## Allianz Stadium: A New Kind of Football Experience

### Why Allianz Stadium Is Different

Opened in **September 2011**, Allianz Stadium (originally Juventus Stadium) was **the first Italian football stadium to be wholly owned by the club that plays in it** — standard practice in England and Germany, revolutionary in a country where clubs traditionally rent municipal grounds.

The result: a stadium **designed purely for football**. No athletics track. Seats positioned close to the pitch. Consistent sightlines from every position. Modern facilities matching west European standards. It is, simply, the best viewing experience of any major Italian club stadium.

**Capacity:** 41,507 (smaller than San Siro, but more intimate and louder in relative terms)  
**Address:** Corso Gaetano Scirea 50, 10151 Torino  
**Built:** 2011  
**Owned by:** Juventus Football Club (not the city)

### San Siro vs. Allianz Stadium: What to Expect

## Getting to Allianz Stadium

**Metro Line 1 (Red)** to **Fermi** or **Paradiso** station (approximately 20 minutes from Turin city centre), then Bus 72 or 63 to the stadium, or a 20–25 minute walk along Via Druento.

**Bus 72** from Torino Porta Nuova (main train station) direct to the stadium — 30 minutes. The most straightforward public transport option from the station.

**Taxi/Uber:** €15–25 from the city centre — fast if you leave before post-match gridlock builds.

**By car:** Club-owned parking (€10–15). More efficient than San Siro. Still, public transport is easier.

**Train from Milan:** Frecciarossa from Milano Centrale to Torino Porta Nuova — **45 minutes**, €20–50. The ideal way to combine a Milan club visit (Inter or AC Milan) with Juventus in Turin on the same trip.

**Arrival timing:** 90 minutes before kick-off for regular Serie A; 2 hours before for Champions League and Derby d'Italia.

## Buying Tickets

### The Nominative System

Juventus uses **nominative (personalised) tickets** — registered to the buyer's ID document, checked at stadium entry. A ticket in another person's name is invalid. Always bring the passport or ID used to register the purchase.

### Where to Buy

- **juventus.com/en/tickets** — the official portal. First choice.
- **TicketOne (ticketone.it)** — Italy's authorised secondary marketplace
- **J1897 Membership** — priority access before public sale

| Tribuna Ovest | €55–100 | €80–140 | €80–130 |

### When Tickets Go on Sale

The Serie A calendar is released in **July**. J1897 members receive priority access. Champions League knock-out matches and derbies sell out fast — membership is the reliable route to tickets for premium fixtures.

**J1897 Membership:** €30–60/year at juventus.com. The single most useful purchase for any serious Juventus football tourist.

### What to Avoid

Viagogo, street sellers: nominative tickets bought through unofficial channels are worthless at the gate. TicketOne's official resale section is the only secondary market to trust.

## Match Day Programme

### Pre-Match (2–3 hours before)

**Juventus Fan Zone** — the plaza outside the stadium comes alive 2–3 hours before kick-off with music, food trucks, fan displays and merchandise stands. More organised and family-oriented than San Siro's pre-match areas; still genuinely atmospheric.

**Official Juventus Store** (stadium and city centre locations) — purchase your official merchandise here before the match, when it's less crowded:
- Current shirt (maglia): €80–120
- Retro replicas (Del Piero 10, Zidane 5, Platini 10): €60–90
- Scarf (sciarpa): €20–30

**Bars near the stadium** — fewer options than Milan's stadium area; the better bars are in the city centre. Recommended approach: aperitivo in central Turin before heading to the stadium.

### Stadium Entry

- Arrive at your designated gate **45 minutes before** kick-off minimum
- **ID + ticket together** at the scanner; they must match
- Allianz Stadium's modern security infrastructure makes processing faster than San Siro
- Prohibited items: large bags (over A4), bottles, cans, umbrellas, selfie sticks
- Transparent bags up to A4 permitted; no outside food or drink

### Curva Sud Torino: The Bianconero Heartbeat

The Curva Sud ultras groups — *Drughi*, *Tradizione Nuova* and others — orchestrate the atmosphere at Allianz Stadium. The style is precise and performative: coordinated chants, elaborate tifo displays, the kind of organised passion that requires weeks of preparation.

Allianz Stadium's **acoustics** are significantly better than San Siro's. The stadium is smaller, seats are closer to the pitch, and the sound focuses rather than dispersing. On a Champions League night with the ground full, the noise level is extraordinary.

**Best for atmosphere:** Curva Sud or Tribuna Sud (adjacent to the Curva)  
**Best for view:** Centre of Tribuna Ovest or Tribuna Est

### Inside the Ground

The match experience at Allianz Stadium is defined by **proximity to the action**. Sitting in the lower tier behind the goal, you are genuinely close to players — you can hear the calls, the instructions, the ball contact. This intimacy is what purpose-built football grounds offer that multi-use venues cannot.

**Photography:** Good natural light for afternoon matches from the eastern stands. For evening matches, the floodlit pitch against the dark sky from the upper Curva sectors gives excellent shots.

### Post-Match

Exit is more efficient than San Siro — wider concourses, better car park access, more experienced crowd management for a single-club venue. Expect 15–25 minutes before your gate clears.

**If Juventus won:** Piazza San Carlo in the city centre (20 minutes away) becomes the gathering point. The Turinese celebration is more restrained than Milan — quieter, more elegant, but genuine.

## Stadium Tour and Juventus Museum

### Museo della Juventus

Located inside Allianz Stadium, the **Juventus Museum** is among the finest football museums in Europe.

**What you'll see:**
- Multiple **Serie A trophies** and the two **Champions League trophies** (1985, 1996)
- Original jerseys: Platini, Baggio, Zidane, Del Piero (shirt #10, retired), Buffon, Ronaldo
- **Interactive simulators** — penalty kick simulator, virtual crowd experience, digital timeline of 125 years
- Historical footage archive: Platini goals, the 1985 European Cup final, Del Piero's masterclass against Deportivo La Coruña (2001)

**Hours:** Daily 10:00–18:00 (closed match days 3 hours before kick-off)  
**Prices:** €18 adults, €12 children  
**Book:** juventus.com/en/museum

### Stadium Tour

**Prices:** €25 adults, €18 children  
**Includes:** VIP tribune, players' dressing rooms, players' tunnel, pitch-side access, panoramic views from upper tiers  
**Duration:** 90 minutes approximately

**Combined museum + tour:** Available at approximately €35/adult — the recommended option for a complete day immersed in Juventus.

## Legendary Players

**Alessandro Del Piero** — shirt number 10, **retired permanently**. 19 seasons at Juventus (1993–2012), 290 goals. When the club was relegated in 2006, Del Piero turned down offers from Bayern, Chelsea and Barcelona to stay in Serie B. This single decision defines what *tifosi* mean when they talk about club loyalty.

**Gianluigi Buffon** — widely considered the greatest goalkeeper in football history. 17 seasons as first-choice at Juventus (2001–2018, with one season at PSG). World record for minutes without conceding. In 2023, he retired at Juventus at 45 years old, completing a full circle.

**Michel Platini** — three consecutive Ballon d'Or awards at Juventus (1983, 1984, 1985). No player has won three in a row since.

**Zinedine Zidane** — his *roulette* turn and chest control are the aesthetic standard against which every technical player in Italian football has since been measured.

**Roberto Baggio** — the Ballon d'Or and the ponytail. Italy's most loved player of the 1990s.

**Pavel Nedvěd** — 2003 Ballon d'Or. The Czech midfielder who drove Juventus's European ambitions in the post-Zidane era with ferocious energy and technical quality.

**Andrea Pirlo** — returning to Juventus late in his career (2011–2017), he added a Ballon d'Or-level performer to a domestic dynasty.

**Cristiano Ronaldo** — 101 goals in 134 appearances. The pursuit of the Champions League that never arrived. Whatever the ultimate verdict, those three seasons produced football of extraordinary individual quality.

## Turin City Guide

### Turin — The Underrated Italian Capital

Turin is Italy's best-kept secret. While Rome and Florence draw millions of tourists and Venice hosts 30 million visitors annually, Turin receives perhaps a tenth of that attention while offering culture, food and architecture that match or exceed any Italian city.

**Historical significance:** Turin was **Italy's first capital** (1861–1865), when the Savoy monarchy unified the peninsula. The city's baroque architecture, elegant piazzas and covered arcade streets (*portici*) — over 18km of covered walkways in the city centre — reflect that royal ambition.

**Industrial identity:** Fiat, Lancia, Olivetti — Turin built the machines that modernised Italy. The Lingotto factory (now an arts and shopping complex) is where the cars were assembled; a test track on the roof featured in the 1969 film *The Italian Job*.

**Gastronomic capital:** Piedmontese cuisine is considered by serious food critics to be the finest regional cuisine in Italy. Truffles (the most expensive ingredient in world gastronomy), exceptional wines (Barolo, Barbaresco, Barbera), and inventions that changed global food culture — vermouth, gianduja chocolate (the ancestor of Nutella) — all come from Piedmont.

### Essential Sights

**Mole Antonelliana** — Turin's defining monument: a 167-metre tower built originally as a synagogue, now housing the **National Cinema Museum**. Panoramic lifts reach the peak (€22 including museum). From the top, the Alps are visible in every direction on a clear day.

**Piazza San Carlo** — the *salotto* (drawing room) of Turin: a baroque piazza ringed by arcades, outdoor cafés and historic buildings. This is where Juventus fans gather to celebrate victories.

**Palazzo Reale and the Holy Shroud** — the former royal palace of the House of Savoy (UNESCO World Heritage Site) houses the Cathedral of San Giovanni Battista, which contains the **Shroud of Turin** — the cloth believed by millions to be the burial shroud of Jesus Christ, the most analysed artefact in history. **€15**.

**Museo Egizio** — the Egyptian Museum contains 40,000 artefacts: the second most important Egyptian collection in the world after Cairo's, including the Papyrus of the Book of the Dead and multiple complete royal statues. **€18**. Book online.

**Lingotto** — Fiat's former factory, now a multi-use complex including the **Pinacoteca Giovanni e Marella Agnelli** (the Agnelli family's private art collection, including Cézanne, Matisse and Picasso — the owners of Juventus). The rooftop test track is open to visitors. **€15** for the gallery.

### Eating in Turin — Piedmontese Cuisine

Piedmontese cooking is a revelation. This is not tourist-adjusted Italian food — it is a regional tradition of exceptional depth and refinement, built on local ingredients that have no equal.

**Vitello tonnato** — cold veal with tuna sauce: the definitive Piedmontese starter, inexplicably delicious.

**Bagna cauda** — hot dip of garlic and anchovies for raw vegetables: a communal winter ritual.

**Agnolotti del plin** — small pasta parcels filled with braised meat, pinched by hand (*plin* means pinch). Utterly different from any pasta you've had elsewhere.

**Tajarin** — thin egg-yolk noodles, typically served with black truffle or a long-cooked meat ragù.

**Truffles** — white truffles from Alba (October–December season) are the most expensive ingredient in world gastronomy. Even mid-range Turinese restaurants serve fresh shaved truffles in season at prices you cannot find anywhere else in the world.

**Bicerin** — Turin's own drink: coffee, hot chocolate and whipped cream served in layers in a small glass at **Caffè Al Bicerin** (Piazza della Consolata 5, open since 1763). Non-optional.

**Vermouth di Torino** — Martini and Cinzano were invented here. A Torino Vermouth with ice and an orange slice is the authentic pre-match ritual.

**Where to eat:**

*Mid-range:*
- **Trattoria Valenza** (Via Borgo Dora 39) — unfussy, authentic Piedmontese classics. €25–35/person
- **Consorzio** (Via Monte di Pietà 23) — modern Piedmontese using natural wines and local producers. €30–45/person

*Splurge:*
- **Del Cambio** (Piazza Carignano 2) — open since 1757, where Count Cavour dined while unifying Italy. Exceptional Piedmontese fine dining. €70–100/person
- **Tre Galline** (Via Bellezia 37) — one of Turin's oldest restaurants, famous for tajarin with truffle. €45–65/person

## Derby d'Italia: The National Rivalry

The **Derby d'Italia** — Juventus vs. Inter Milan — is not a city derby; it is the supreme national rivalry of Italian football. Two clubs from different cities, different regions, and different identities that have consistently competed at the apex of Italian football for over a century.

### What Makes It Different

Unlike the Derby della Madonnina (same city, same stadium), the Derby d'Italia pits:
- **Turin** (industrial, northwestern, Agnelli dynasty, working-class roots) against **Milan** (cosmopolitan, financial capital, international)
- **Black and white** against **blue and black**
- The serial Italian champions against the self-proclaimed *Internazionale* (international) club

### The Match in Context

In many seasons, Derby d'Italia effectively decides the Serie A title. The record in direct encounters is close enough that no club has ever properly dominated the rivalry. Each edition carries the weight of the title race.

### Getting Tickets

Derby d'Italia matches at Allianz Stadium sell extremely quickly. J1897 membership priority access is essential. TicketOne's resale section occasionally offers returned tickets in the days before the match.

## 8 Insider Tips

**1. The Juventus Museum is the best football museum in Italy.** The interactive installations, trophy collection and historical depth put it ahead of Milan's Casa Milan and Inter's smaller museum. Allocate three hours.

**2. Eat vitello tonnato and drink a Bicerin on the same day.** These two are Turin's gastronomic signatures and neither exists in quite the same form anywhere else. Caffè Al Bicerin has been serving since 1763.

**3. Del Piero's number 10 is retired — say so.** Mentioning this to any Juventus fan signals genuine knowledge and will immediately open a conversation. The decision to stay in Serie B is the club's most important moment in the past 20 years.

**4. Train from Milan beats flying to Turin.** 45 minutes on Frecciarossa, central station in the heart of the city, no luggage restrictions. If you're in Italy already, this is the only sensible way to reach Turin.

**5. Truffle season is October–December.** If you can arrange your trip during these months, even a mid-range trattoria serves fresh-shaved white truffle from Alba over pasta. This is not a regular food experience — it's among the ten best things you can eat in Europe.

**6. Allianz Stadium acoustics on a Champions League night.** The stadium's size and design focus the crowd noise with remarkable effectiveness. On a full-house European night, the volume is physically impressive. Choose Champions League over Serie A if your dates allow.

**7. Piazza San Carlo for the post-match celebration.** Turin's celebrations are quieter than Milan's — more Turinese in character — but the Piazza San Carlo aperitivo bars after a Juventus victory have a particular quality of restrained delight.

**8. Book Lingotto alongside the Juventus Museum.** The former Fiat factory explains the Agnelli-Juventus relationship better than any words. The Pinacoteca (the Agnelli family art collection) is world-class. And the rooftop test track from the 1969 Italian Job film is unforgettable.

## Final Thoughts: Why Turin and Juventus?

Watching Juventus at Allianz Stadium is a different experience from San Siro. Less grandiose, more precise. A modern ground built with the single purpose of creating the ideal football-watching environment, in a city that takes its football as seriously as any in Europe.

Turin itself rewards the effort to get there: better food than Milan, fewer tourists than Rome, architecture as impressive as any Italian city, and a gastronomic tradition (truffle, Barolo, Bicerin, vermouth) that is genuinely world-class.

The combination of 36 Scudetti, Platini, Zidane, Del Piero, Buffon, Ronaldo, and a museum that does justice to all of them, makes Allianz Stadium one of the essential stops on any Italian football tourism itinerary.

**Fino alla Fine. Forza Juve.**

## Extending Your Italian Football Trip

### The Complete Northern Italy Football Circuit

Turin and Milan are 45 minutes apart by Frecciarossa high-speed train. A well-planned week can include three or even four Serie A matches:

**Day 1:** Arrive Milan (Bergamo BGY via Ryanair)  
**Day 2–3:** Inter Milan or AC Milan match at San Siro  
**Day 4:** Frecciarossa to Turin (45 min) — explore city, Bicerin at Caffè Al Bicerin  
**Day 5–6:** Juventus match at Allianz Stadium + Juventus Museum  
**Day 7:** Return from Turin TRN or back through Milan

This itinerary is one of European football's great pilgrimages: two cities, up to three matches, two of Italy's finest regional cuisines, some of the continent's most storied clubs.

**Atalanta option:** Bergamo's Atalanta (Gewiss Stadium, capacity 21,747) is 45 minutes from Milan by train. One of the most exciting teams in Italy over the past decade, with a passionate atmosphere and tickets significantly easier to obtain than San Siro. Adding an Atalanta match extends the circuit without additional travel.

### Combining Champions League Weeks

If Juventus, Inter and Milan are all in the Champions League in the same season (common in recent years), the mid-week European fixtures open up remarkable possibilities: Tuesday Inter at San Siro, Wednesday Juventus at Allianz Stadium (or vice versa). Frecciarossa between the two cities takes 45 minutes. The cost of an additional night's accommodation is the only additional expense.

## Practical Italian Football Tourist Tips

**Lesson 1: Calendar planning is everything.** The Serie A calendar appears in July; Champions League group stage fixtures in late August. Block your calendar immediately for the dates you want. Ticket sales follow within weeks.

**Lesson 2: Every Italian ground uses ID-linked tickets.** Carry your passport or national ID to every match. This is non-negotiable, strictly enforced, and represents the single most common mistake by first-time visitors to Italian football.

**Lesson 3: Arrive early, leave late.** Italian football pre-match culture — the gathering, the aperitivo, the songs outside the ground — is part of the experience. A ticket for kick-off that gets you to the stadium 10 minutes before misses half the story.

**Lesson 4: Learn the chants.** Five words of Italian and basic familiarity with the club's key songs transform you from spectator to participant. Apps, YouTube, the official club app: all provide versions. Effort is recognised and welcomed.

**Lesson 5: Post-match eating is the reward.** Italian restaurants serve until 23:00–00:30 in most cities. A 20:45 match ends around 22:30. Half an hour later you're in a trattoria. This is the correct order of the Italian football day.

**Lesson 6: Insurance is not optional for match trips.** Matches are postponed. Flights are delayed. Hotels cancel. A trip built around a specific fixture on a specific date carries more risk than a flexible holiday. EKTA and equivalent providers offer sports-event-specific policies that cover these scenarios.
