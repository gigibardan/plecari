Belgrade doesn't explain itself — it's experienced.

It's the only European city where you can drink a beer at a floating river club at 4 AM, eat grilled ćevapi two hours earlier and watch the sunrise from the walls of a Roman fortress that's 2,000 years old. All in the same weekend, all for under 50 euros.

It's not an agreeable city in the conventional tourist sense — it doesn't have Prague's perfect cleanliness or Vienna's elegance. It's raw, noisy, contradictory, with NATO-bombed buildings from 1999 standing abandoned next to communist blocks and brand-new boutique hotels. But it has an energy few European cities still possess — that of a place that has bounced back from wars, sanctions and isolation and chosen to party.

## Belgrade at a Glance — Essential Facts

**Country:** Serbia — not an EU member, not in Schengen.

**Population:** approximately 1.7 million (metropolitan area) — the largest city in the Balkans after Istanbul.

**Currency:** Serbian Dinar (RSD). 1 EUR ≈ 117 RSD in 2026. **Serbia does not use the euro** — you need to exchange currency.

**Language:** Serbian is the official language (Cyrillic and Latin alphabets). English is well understood in the tourist area and by younger generations.

**Public transport:** **free** since 2025 — bus, tram, trolleybus throughout Belgrade.

**Visa:** EU citizens do not need a visa for Serbia — entry with a valid ID card or passport.

**Direct flights from Bucharest:** Air Serbia, OTP→BEG, 45–55 minutes.

## How to Get from Romania to Belgrade

### Direct Flight (45 Minutes — Fastest Option)

Air Serbia operates direct flights Bucharest (OTP) → Belgrade (BEG). Duration: 45–55 minutes — one of the shortest international flights in Europe.

**Indicative 2026 prices:** 30–90 EUR return with 2–6 weeks' advance booking. Sometimes available at 25–30 EUR return on promotion.

**Airport → city centre transfer:** Bus A1 (free since 2025!) → Slavija Square: 30 minutes. Taxi/Bolt: 12–18 EUR, 20–25 minutes.

### By Car (Recommended for Flexibility)

Distance Bucharest → Belgrade: ~400 km, 4–4.5 hours on motorway. **Important: Serbia is not in the EU** — you need your vehicle documents, a Green Card (international insurance) and ID/passport.

### By Coach

FlixBus and Romanian operators offer direct routes Bucharest → Belgrade. Duration: 7–8 hours, prices: 20–40 EUR. The budget option, less comfortable.

## The Serbian Dinar — Quick Guide for Tourists

Serbia doesn't use the euro. You'll pay in **Serbian dinars (RSD)**.

**2026 exchange rate:** 1 EUR ≈ 117 RSD.

**Quick calculation:**
- 500 RSD ≈ 4.30 EUR
- 1,000 RSD ≈ 8.50 EUR
- 5,000 RSD ≈ 43 EUR

**Where to exchange:** local ATMs (best rate, small commission), authorised exchange offices in the centre. **Avoid** airport exchange (unfavourable rate) and street exchanges (scam risk).

Cards are accepted in most restaurants and hotels, but many small local places and markets are cash-only.

## What to See in Belgrade

### Kalemegdan Fortress — 2,000 Years of Free History

Kalemegdan is Belgrade's soul. The Roman-medieval-Ottoman-Austro-Hungarian fortress rises on a hill 125 metres above the confluence of the Sava and Danube — a vantage point that explains why, throughout history, 40 different armies have conquered and lost this place.

**Park and walls access: free, open 24/7.** In the evening, at sunset, it's one of Belgrade's most spectacular moments — locals come with supermarket wine and sit on the parapets to watch the sun set over the river confluence.

**The Victor Monument (Pobednik)** — Ivan Meštrović's 14-metre bronze statue, Belgrade's symbol. It faces west, with its back to the city — according to legend, because the Serbs didn't know who the next enemy would be.

**Military Museum:** entry ~4 EUR, excellent for historical context.

**Nebojša Tower:** Ottoman prison where Greek revolutionary poet Rigas Feraios was executed in 1798. Entry ~3 EUR.

### Knez Mihailova — The Main Boulevard

The pedestrian street Knez Mihailova (Prince Mihailo) is the spine of central Belgrade — 1.5 km from Republic Square to the Kalemegdan gate. Flanked by neoclassical and eclectic 19th-century buildings, it's full of cafés, shops and street performers. Turkish coffee (kafa) at an authentic local café: 1–1.50 EUR.

**Republic Square (Trg Republike)** — the city's meeting point, with the equestrian statue of Prince Mihailo. Locals say "see you kod konja" (at the horse) — you can use the expression too; Serbs appreciate it.

### Skadarlija — The Bohemian Quarter with Kafanas and Live Music

Skadarlija is the quarter with which Belgrade compares itself to Montmartre — a cobblestone street flanked by kafanas (traditional Serbian taverns) with live tamburica music, aromatic stews and rakija served in small glasses.

The major kafanas: **Tri Šešira** (Three Hats) and **Dva Jelena** (Two Deer) — historic, touristy, but authentic in food and music. A full meal with live music and beer: 15–25 EUR. Live music charge: sometimes 3–5 EUR separately — ask before sitting down.

**Tip:** Skadarlija is more authentic Tuesday–Thursday evening. Weekends attract large tourist groups. For the real local experience, come at lunchtime — kafanas serve daily menus for 4–7 EUR.

### Temple of Saint Sava — The World's Largest Orthodox Church

The Temple of Saint Sava is colossal — the second largest by floor area in the world (after the converted Hagia Sophia), with a 70-metre dome visible from kilometres away. Construction began in 1935 and continues today (the interior mosaics were recently completed).

Entry is free. The crypt below (more recently completed, with impressive Byzantine mosaics) is worth 15–20 minutes.

### Savamala — The Creative District and Underground Clubs

Savamala is what happened when artists and entrepreneurs took over an abandoned industrial neighbourhood on the Sava riverbank. Today it's the epicentre of Belgrade's alternative culture: art galleries, design studios, hipster cafés, concept restaurants and — most importantly — legendary underground clubs.

**Klub Drugstore, Mladost i zdravlje, 20/44** — clubs in former warehouses and factories, electronic music, running Friday–Saturday until morning. Entry: free or 5–10 EUR.

**Mikser House** — former furniture factory, now a cultural space with cafés, galleries and events. Free during the day.

### The Splavs — Belgrade's Unique Riverside Nightlife

The splavs are one of Belgrade's most original experiences — floating structures moored on the banks of the Sava and Danube, operating as restaurants by day and clubs by night.

**Ada Huja area** (on the Danube) and **the Sava zone** (below Kalemegdan) are the main clusters.

Two distinct categories:

**Restaurant/bar splavs** (open from 10:00): Serbian food, water-view terraces, cheap beer, relaxed atmosphere. Prices similar to city centre restaurants. Perfect for lunch or a sunset cocktail.

**Club splavs** (open after midnight, running until 7:00–8:00 AM): electronic music (techno, house) or turbo-folk (popular Balkan music genre, controversial but authentic). Entry: free to 15 EUR. Minimum spend sometimes required.

**Essential tip:** Belgraders don't start their evening before midnight. If you arrive at a club at 11 PM, you're one of the first. The peak is 2:00–5:00 AM.

## Food and Drink — Belgrade Gastronomy Guide

### What You Must Eat

**Ćevapi (ćevapčići)** — Balkan grilled sausages made from mixed minced meat (beef+pork or beef+lamb), served with flatbread (lepinje), onion and ajvar (red pepper paste). A 10-piece portion: 600–900 RSD (5–8 EUR). Best at a roštilj (grill restaurant), not fast food.

**Pljeskavica** — the Serbian burger — an enormous flat spiced minced meat patty served in lepinje with toppings. At street stalls: 400–600 RSD (3–5 EUR). At good restaurants: the "punjena" version (stuffed with cheese and kajmak) at 700–1,000 RSD.

**Kajmak** — fermented milk cream, somewhere between sour cream and butter, served as a side with almost everything. Do not leave Belgrade without tasting fresh kajmak.

**Burek** — filo pastry pie (a savoury baklava), filled with cheese, meat or spinach, baked in large trays and sold by the slice. At local bakeries: 150–250 RSD (1.30–2.20 EUR) a slice. Classic Belgrade breakfast with yoghurt.

**Karađorđeva šnicla** — Serbia's national schnitzel, rolled with kajmak and ham. 900–1,400 RSD (7–12 EUR) at a restaurant.

### Rakija — The National Drink

Rakija is the drink with which Serbia most strongly identifies — a fruit brandy (plum = šljivovica, quince = dunjevača, apricot = kajsijevača) at 40–65% ABV, consumed at any time of day and in any social context.

At kafanas, rakija is served in small glasses (50 ml) at 150–400 RSD (1.30–3.50 EUR). Serbian hosts will offer you homemade rakija — it's a hospitality gesture; polite refusal is acceptable but honest appreciation of the drink's quality is extremely well received.

### Where to Eat

**Authentic budget (3–8 EUR/meal):**
- **Roštilji** (grill restaurants) in popular neighbourhoods Zemun, Vračar
- **Bakeries** for burek and gibanica at breakfast
- **Zeleni Venac Market** — the central market with fresh produce and food stalls

**Mid-range (8–20 EUR/person):**
- **Dva Jelena** (Skadarlija) — historic kafana, live music, traditional food
- **Restoran Šešir Moj** (Skadarlija) — authentic atmosphere, full menu
- **Savamala cafés** — for something more contemporary

## Practical Tips for Belgrade

**Public transport is free** — since 2025, all buses, trams and trolleybuses in Belgrade are free. No ticket needed. The BusPlus app shows routes and real-time arrivals.

**Taxis: use an app** — CarGo (Serbian local app) or Bolt are the safest and cheapest. Street taxis can charge inflated prices for tourists.

**ATMs:** best rates at Raiffeisen, Banca Intesa or UniCredit ATMs. Withdraw larger amounts at once (fixed fee per withdrawal, not percentage).

**Live music charge** — at Skadarlija kafanas, live music sometimes means an additional charge of 300–500 RSD per person. Ask BEFORE sitting down.

**NATO-bombed buildings** — in central Belgrade, some buildings remain undemolished and unreconstruted from the 1999 bombings. Avoid photographing them from close up — there are restrictions and locals can react negatively.
