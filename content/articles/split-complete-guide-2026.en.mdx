Split is the city where you walk down a street and realise the walls on either side are 1,700 years old. Not in the sense of visiting a ruin with a ticket and audio guide — in the sense that laundry hangs on a Roman wall, cats sleep on Corinthian columns, and the café where you drink cappuccino at 10 in the morning is literally in the basement of an imperial palace.

Diocletian — the only Roman emperor to voluntarily abdicate — built his retirement palace here, on the Dalmatian coast, between 295 and 305 AD. It was a 215 × 180 m fortress with 2 m-thick walls, 20 m towers, and a sea view that hasn't changed in 17 centuries. After the empire fell, people from Salona (the Roman city on the hill, destroyed by Avars) took refuge in the palace and turned imperial chambers into homes, temples into churches, and the central square into a marketplace. And so it remained. Today, 3,000 people live within the palace walls — the oldest continuously inhabited ancient site in the world.

## Diocletian's Palace — Not a Museum, a City

If you expect a museum experience, Split will surprise you. The palace has no single entry ticket or access gate — you walk through one of four gates (Golden/north, Silver/east, Iron/west, Bronze/south/sea) and you're in the middle of a city that has functioned for 17 centuries without interruption.

**The Peristyle:** The palace's central courtyard — an open space with Corinthian columns of red granite brought from Egypt. Today it hosts cafés (Luxor — drink coffee on the steps where the imperial guard stood), occasional performances, and in the evening, when lights hit the columns, it's magical. Free, permanently open.

**The Basement Halls (Podrumi):** The palace cellars — the world's best-preserved Roman substructures. The vaulting, columns, and original layout are intact. Part now functions as a souvenir market (history's irony), but the museum sections are impressive. Entry: €12. Worth it — they give the real perspective of the palace's scale.

**Cathedral of St Domnius (Katedrala Sv. Duje):** Built directly into Diocletian's octagonal mausoleum — supreme irony, since Diocletian was the greatest persecutor of Christians in Roman history. The interior preserves original columns, reliefs, and an excellent medieval altar. The bell tower (57 m) offers the best city panorama — €7 to climb (narrow steps, worth it). Cathedral entry: €5.

**Temple of Jupiter:** Small, intact, converted into a baptistery. Jupiter's original statue was replaced with a crucifix. The black granite sphinx at the entrance is authentic — brought from Egypt by Diocletian.

**Golden Gate (Zlatna Vrata):** The northern gate, the most imposing. Outside, the massive statue of Bishop Grgur Ninski by Ivan Meštrović — touch the big toe of his left foot for luck. It's shiny from wear.

## Riva and Street Life

**Riva** (Obala Hrvatskog Narodnog Preporoda) is the seafront promenade directly below the palace's southern wall — terraces with palm trees, cafés, gelaterias, and views toward the harbour and the islands of Brač and Hvar in the distance. It's Split's collective living room: morning — coffee. Afternoon — gelato. Evening — cocktails. Night — beer and conversation.

**Note:** Riva restaurants are beautiful but expensive (€15–25/main course) and quality is mediocre — they survive on location. The real food is on the back streets (Šperun, Varoš, Lučac).

## Marjan — The Green Lung

Marjan hill (178 m) is a pine-covered peninsula west of the centre — 15 minutes' walk from the Riva. It has hiking trails, hidden beaches on the western coast (Kašjuni, Bene, Jezinac), a medieval chapel (Sv. Jere — with a spectacular path cut into rock), and a summit viewpoint with 360° panorama of Split, the islands, and the Mosor mountains.

**Best route:** From Riva, climb the stairs from Varoš (the old fishermen's quarter with stone houses) → Sv. Jere chapel (15 min) → Telegrin viewpoint (30 min from chapel) → descent on the western slope to Kašjuni beach → return via the coastal road. Total: 2–3 hours, easy difficulty.

## Islands from Split — Day Trips

**Hvar (1–2 hours by catamaran/ferry):** Hvar town is a Venetian gem — yacht harbour, cathedral square, the Spanish Fortica fortress on the hill (€8, spectacular panorama), and lavender fields in the interior (June is blooming season). Nightlife is surprisingly lively. The Pakleni Islands: an archipelago 20 min by boat — hidden beaches, cliff-side restaurants (Laganini on Palmižana — fish with feet in the water, €15–25/main), excellent snorkelling. Water taxi: €10–15 return.

**Brač (50 min by catamaran to Bol):** **Zlatni Rat (Golden Horn):** A horn-shaped beach extending 300 m into the sea that shifts shape with currents and wind — one of Europe's most iconic beaches. Fine white pebble, turquoise water, ideal for windsurfing. Crowded in summer but its length makes it bearable. Bol village behind the beach is charming: stone houses, small harbour, fish restaurants.

**Vis (2.5 hours by ferry):** The furthest accessible island from Split — and the least touristy. Vis was Yugoslavia's military base until 1989 and only opened to tourism since. Result: an almost unmodified island with spectacular beaches (Stiniva — tiny cove between 50 m cliffs, voted Europe's most beautiful beach in 2016), excellent wines, and an atmosphere reminiscent of Greece 30 years ago. "Mamma Mia: Here We Go Again" was filmed on Vis.

**Trogir (30 min by bus 37):** Not an island but a UNESCO town on a tiny islet connected by bridge. The old town is a compact medieval labyrinth — the cathedral with Radovan's 13th-century Romanesque-Gothic portal, Kamerlengo fortress with sunset views, and stone-house lanes. Less crowded than Split and more photogenic. Ideal half-day trip.

## Food — Dalmatia on the Table

**Pršut (Dalmatian prosciutto):** Dry-cured ham aged in the Dalmatian wind — smokier and more intense than Italian. A plate with pršut, sheep cheese (paški sir from Pag island), and olives: €8–12. Perfect with a glass of pošip (white wine from Korčula).

**Peka:** Dalmatia's signature dish — meat (veal or octopus) with potatoes and vegetables, cooked under a metal dome covered in embers. Prepared for 2–3 hours. **Must be ordered at least 2 hours in advance** (or at lunch for dinner). Konoba Hvaranin (Šperun) and Konoba Matejuška (harbour) make excellent peka. €15–22/person.

**Fish and seafood:** Everywhere on the coast, but the best are at konobe (tavernas) in the Varoš and Radunica quarters, not on the Riva. Grilled sea bream: €12–18. Buzara mussels/clams (in white wine and garlic sauce): €10–14. Octopus salad: €8–10.

**Where to eat:**

**Konoba Hvaranin (Šperun):** Probably Split's best konoba — legendary octopus peka, fresh fish. Small room, reservation essential. €15–25/person.

**Villa Spiza (old town):** Daily-changing menu written on a chalkboard. Generous portions, local ingredients. Popular — come at 12:00 or 18:00 to get a seat. €10–18/person.

**Konoba Matejuška (near the fishing harbour):** Morning's fish, quayside terrace. €12–20/person. No pretension, excellent quality.

## Essential Tips

**Cruise ships ruin the experience.** In July–August, 2–4 cruise ships anchor daily in Split, pouring 5,000–10,000 passengers into the old town. The Peristyle becomes impassable 10:00–16:00. Check cruisemapper.com for the schedule and plan visits on ship-free days. Alternative: early morning (08:00–09:00) or evening.

**Varoš is the real neighbourhood.** The hill west of the palace, with stone houses on steep streets, laundry on lines between windows, and cats on stairs. Zero tourists compared to the palace interior. Walk without destination — it's Split's most beautiful quarter.

**Peka must be ordered in advance.** Two–three hours minimum. Ask at check-in which konoba makes good peka and order at lunch for dinner. Worth the wait.

**Bačvice and picigin.** Bačvice beach (10 min from centre) is where locals play picigin — a traditional Split game where teams keep a small ball in the air without letting it drop, standing in knee-deep water. It's a sport, a social ritual, and a spectacle. Played since 1900.

**Croatia joined Schengen and the eurozone in 2023.** No kuna needed — everything is in euros. Cards work everywhere.
