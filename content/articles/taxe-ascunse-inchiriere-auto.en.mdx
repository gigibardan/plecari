The price you see online for a car rental is not the price you will pay. The difference comes from fees, insurance products and add-ons that appear only at the counter — or buried in the fine print of the contract. Here are the most common ones, with the numbers, and how to avoid each.

## CDW and the excess (the industry's most expensive "free" thing)

Almost every rental includes CDW (Collision Damage Waiver) — the basic damage insurance. It sounds good, but it comes with an **excess**: the amount you pay out of pocket if the car is damaged. This can be anywhere between €500 and €2,000, and it is blocked on your card as a deposit at collection.

What happens in practice: you scratch the bumper in a car park → you pay €800 from your card → the company repairs the bumper for €150 → the difference stays with them. This is legal, because you signed the contract with the excess clause.

**How to avoid it:** buy a **third-party excess waiver** (insurance4carhire, iCarhireinsurance, or RentalCover) before collecting the car. It costs €3–8 per day instead of €15–30 at the counter, and it reduces your excess to zero. The counter staff will push their own product — politely decline.

## The airport surcharge (10–15% extra)

Rental companies pay a concession fee to the airport for access to the terminal. You end up paying it, labelled as an "airport surcharge" — 10–15% added to the total, typically not shown prominently in the initial search results.

**How to avoid it:** collect from a **city-centre location** instead of the airport. Get there by Uber or taxi (€10–20) and save €50–100+ on the surcharge for a week's rental. Some companies even offer a free shuttle from the terminal to their off-airport lot.

The maths: if your rental is €300 for a week, the airport surcharge adds €30–45. The Uber to the city location costs €12. You save €18–33 net, plus you avoid the often chaotic airport car-rental queue.

## Young driver fee (under 25)

Under 25 years old? Add €5–15 per day. On a 10-day rental, that is €50–150 purely for being the "wrong" age.

**How to avoid it:** realistically, you cannot with most major chains. Some smaller or local companies do not charge it — worth comparing on a platform that shows the true total price (including the young driver fee) rather than just the base rate.

Some credit cards with car rental benefits waive the young driver fee — check your card's terms.

## GPS: €70–105 per week for something your phone already does

The in-car GPS at the counter costs €10–15 per day. On a 7-day rental, that is €70–105. Your phone does exactly the same thing, for free.

**How to avoid it:** use Google Maps or Waze on your phone. If you are travelling outside the EU and do not want to use roaming data, get a local eSIM with data for €5–10 (see [best eSIM by destination](/en/esim-roaming/cel-mai-bun-esim-per-destinatie)), or download offline maps for your destination before you leave.

If you genuinely want a physical mount, a phone holder costs €10 on Amazon. That is your one-time investment.

## The fuel policy trap

There are two main fuel policies. Understand the difference before signing:

| Policy | How it works | What you actually pay |
|---|---|---|
| Full-to-full | Collect full, return full | Only what you used, at petrol station prices |
| Pre-purchase fuel | Pay for a full tank upfront (at company prices), return empty | More than you use — you can never empty it exactly |
| Full-to-empty | Collect full, return empty — fuel included in price | Built into the rental price, usually expensive |

**Always choose full-to-full.** The pre-purchase option sounds convenient but you always lose fuel you paid for — nobody returns a car with a perfectly empty tank. The company keeps the surplus.

A practical note on returning full: find a petrol station within 2–3 km of the drop-off point when you are navigating to the return location. Returning the car even 10% below full with a full-to-full contract can result in a surcharge of €20–40 at the company's higher per-litre rate.

## Additional charges to watch for

Beyond the major ones, a full contract can include:

**One-way fee:** Collecting in Split, returning in Dubrovnik, or collecting at Heathrow and returning at Gatwick — many companies charge €30–80+ for this. Some include one-way within the same country for free. Always check at booking, not at the counter.

**Cross-border fee:** Driving from Italy into Slovenia, or from Greece into Albania — many rental contracts prohibit this or charge €10–30 per country, plus a requirement for additional insurance. If your trip involves multiple countries, declare this at booking and verify it is allowed.

**Mileage limits:** Some cheap tariffs include a cap of 200–300 km per day, with excess charged at €0.20–0.50 per km. This is rarely an issue for city rentals but becomes significant on road trips. Check the daily mileage allowance before you book.

**Child seats and accessories:** €5–15 per day each. If you need a child seat for a 10-day trip, that is €50–150 — worth buying a lightweight travel seat (€25–50) if you travel with young children regularly.

**Toll charges:** some companies rent a toll transponder for €5–15/day rather than you paying individually. On routes with few tolls it is not worth it. On routes like the French péage system or Croatian motorways, check the maths.

**Insurance for tyres, windscreen and undercarriage:** these are often not covered by basic CDW and are sold separately at €3–8/day each. A third-party excess waiver from a specialist (mentioned above) often covers these automatically — another reason the specialist product beats the counter product.

## The counter script (and how to respond)

Here is what the counter agent will say, and the appropriate response:

**"I see your booking includes basic insurance only — for just €X per day I can upgrade you to our Full Protection."**
→ "No thank you, I have my own excess insurance arranged."

**"We are low on your car category — would you like to upgrade for €Y per day?"**
→ "I will take what I booked. If you need to upgrade me because of unavailability, there is no additional charge."

**"Would you like to pre-purchase your fuel? Then you can return the car empty and not worry."**
→ "No, I will use full-to-full. Can you confirm the tank is full now?"

**"Our GPS is very useful in this region — just €Z per day."**
→ "No thank you, I will use my phone."

Being prepared for these questions takes the pressure off. The counter agent is not your enemy — they have upsell targets — but you are not obliged to buy anything beyond what you booked.

## How to compare car rental prices properly

The price shown in the initial search results on most platforms is the base rate — before airport surcharges, taxes, young driver fees, and mandatory insurance add-ons. Here is how to compare properly:

1. **Search on a comparison platform** (GetRentacar, DiscoverCars, Rentalcars) that shows the total price including mandatory fees
2. **Set your age correctly** — this triggers the young driver fee where applicable
3. **Enter the correct collection location** — airport vs. city centre gives you different prices
4. **Check the fuel policy** before comparing — two prices can only be compared if the fuel policy is the same
5. **Read the included insurance** — is CDW included? What is the excess?
6. **Factor in your own excess insurance** (€3–8/day from a specialist) to get the true comparison

The cheapest initial price is very rarely the cheapest final cost.

## Before you collect: a practical checklist

- [ ] Print or download the confirmation with booking reference
- [ ] Have your third-party excess insurance confirmation ready
- [ ] Bring the credit card used at booking (most companies require the same card)
- [ ] At collection: photograph every panel of the car, all four sides, plus roof and wheels
- [ ] Send the photos to yourself by email immediately — timestamped proof
- [ ] Verify the fuel level on the contract matches actual (full or noted level)
- [ ] Confirm the mileage shown on the odometer matches the contract
- [ ] Get the direct number for the rental company's roadside assistance before driving away

Photographing the car thoroughly at collection is the single most important protection you have. Damage disputes happen — having timestamped photos from before you drove eliminates them.
