Istanbul does not ease you in gently. The moment you step out of the airport — or better, the moment you take the ferry across the Bosphorus for the first time and watch the minarets rising above both the European and Asian skylines simultaneously — you understand why this city has been the hinge of the world for 2,500 years. It is the only city on two continents, and it holds layers of history — Byzantine, Ottoman, secular, modern — with a density that no other European destination can match.

It is also remarkably affordable, relatively safe, and genuinely exhilarating for a first-time visitor.

## The one thing you must do before you leave home

**Buy your eSIM before departure.** This cannot be stated strongly enough.

Turkey is outside the EU. Free roaming does not apply. Your home carrier charges €10–15 per gigabyte in Turkey. A week of normal data use (maps, messages, a few photos uploaded) costs €50–100 in carrier roaming.

The solution — a prepaid Turkey eSIM — costs $10–15 for 5 GB / 30 days on [Airalo](https://airalo.tpo.mx/mYekFAo3). But here is the critical detail: since 2025, Turkish authorities have blocked access to eSIM provider websites from within Turkey. If you arrive without one and try to buy it at the hotel, you will find the pages inaccessible.

Buy before you fly. Install at home. It activates automatically when you land. Full installation instructions: [how to install an eSIM](/en/esim-roaming/how-to-install-esim).

## 3-Day Istanbul itinerary

### Day 1 — Sultanahmet: the old city

Sultanahmet is the historic heart of Istanbul — the peninsula where Constantinople stood, surrounded on three sides by water. The density of significant monuments within walking distance of each other here is unmatched in the world.

**Hagia Sophia (Ayasofya)**

Built in 537 CE as the greatest cathedral in Christendom, converted to a mosque after the Ottoman conquest in 1453, secularised as a museum under Atatürk, and reconverted to a mosque in 2020. The interior — 55 metres high, 270 metres in circumference, covered in gold mosaics that have survived 1,500 years — is staggering under any description.

Arrival: open from 8:30 AM. Come at opening — by 10 AM the interior is crowded and the experience is diminished. Access during prayer times (five times daily, 20–40 minutes each) is restricted. Entry is free for prayer; tourist access through a separate entrance may carry a charge — verify the current arrangement on the Hagia Sophia's official site before visiting.

**Blue Mosque (Sultan Ahmed Mosque)**

Directly across the Hippodrome from Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque is named for the 20,000 İznik tiles in varying shades of blue that cover its interior. Its six minarets caused controversy when built in 1616 (only Mecca was meant to have six). Free to enter; closed to tourists during the five daily prayer times (30 minutes each). Dress code enforced at the entrance — shoulders and knees covered, women need a headscarf (provided free at the entrance if needed).

**Topkapi Palace**

The administrative and residential centre of the Ottoman Empire for four centuries (1465–1856). The complex covers 700,000 square metres and contains the Imperial Council chambers, treasury collections, holy relics, and — most fascinating — the Harem, where the sultan's household and family lived in an interlocking world of power, privacy, and protocol.

Allow 3–4 hours minimum. The Harem requires a separate ticket. Entrance: approximately €20 for the main complex, €15 for the Harem.

**The Basilica Cistern (Yerebatan Sarnıcı)**

A 6th-century underground water reservoir — 336 columns supporting a cathedral-like space that held 80,000 cubic metres of water for the city above. Atmospheric and slightly surreal. Walk the raised platforms among the columns and find the two Medusa heads used as column bases (one upside down, one sideways — a detail that has generated centuries of speculation). Entry approximately €15. Open daily.

**The Hippodrome**

The open square in front of the Blue Mosque is the site of the ancient chariot racing track of Constantinople, once seating 100,000 spectators. The Egyptian Obelisk (3,500 years old, moved here from Karnak in 390 CE), the Serpentine Column (from Delphi, 479 BCE), and the Column of Constantine stand on what was once the track's central spine. Free, always open.

**Where to eat on Day 1:** avoid the tourist-facing restaurants immediately around the Hippodrome and Blue Mosque — quality is poor and prices are high. Walk 3–4 minutes into the streets behind them. Simit (sesame bread ring, €0.50) from street vendors for breakfast. A lokanta (Turkish canteen-style restaurant) for lunch — a full meal with soup, main, and water costs €5–8.

### Day 2 — Grand Bazaar, Galata and the Bosphorus

**Grand Bazaar (Kapalıçarşı)**

One of the world's oldest and largest covered markets, operating continuously since 1461. Over 4,000 shops under 61 covered streets, selling jewellery, carpets, ceramics, leather, spices, textiles, and souvenirs. The scale is disorienting and the experience is genuinely unlike any other market in Europe.

The negotiation culture is real but not aggressive. For souvenirs and handicrafts, starting at 50% of the first price and meeting at 60–70% is typical. For carpets and larger items, prices are more complex — treat any carpet purchase as a multi-hour negotiation and research values beforehand.

Opening hours: Monday to Saturday, 8:30 AM – 7 PM. Closed Sunday.

**Spice Bazaar (Mısır Çarşısı)**

A smaller, more focused market near the waterfront — primarily spices, teas, Turkish delight (lokum), dried fruits, and nuts. Less overwhelming than the Grand Bazaar and more enjoyable for food lovers. Try the saffron, the pomegranate tea, and the pistachio lokum.

**Galata Bridge and the Karaköy waterfront**

The Galata Bridge crosses the Golden Horn inlet connecting the old city to the Beyoğlu district. The bridge's lower level is lined with restaurants; the upper level is perpetually occupied by Istanbulites fishing — one of the city's most iconic sights. Walk across toward the Karaköy neighbourhood, which has transformed in the past decade into Istanbul's most dynamic area for food, coffee, and contemporary art.

**Galata Tower**

The 67-metre stone tower built in 1348 by the Genoese offers a 360-degree panorama of Istanbul — the old city, the Bosphorus, the Asian shore, and the Horn. Entry approximately €30. Best visited in late afternoon when the light on the domes and minarets is at its most dramatic.

**Bosphorus cruise**

The Bosphorus Strait — 31 km connecting the Black Sea to the Marmara Sea, separating Europe from Asia — is the defining geographical feature of Istanbul and best understood from the water.

| Option | Duration | Price | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Public ferry (Istanbulkart) | 90 minutes (one way) | €1–2 | Budget, locals' experience |
| Short Bosphorus tour | 1.5–2 hours | €15–25 | Tourist-focused, commentary |
| Full day cruise | 5–6 hours | €25–40 | Reaching the Black Sea entrance |
| Sunset dinner cruise | 2–3 hours | €40–70 | Romantic, food included |

The public ferry from Eminönü to Üsküdar (6 minutes, €1) gives you a Bosphorus crossing for essentially nothing. The longer cruise boats depart from the same Eminönü pier and go significantly further north, passing waterfront palaces, fortresses, and the suspension bridges.

### Day 3 — Asian Istanbul and the Turkish hammam

**Kadıköy (Asian side)**

Take the ferry from Eminönü or Karaköy to Kadıköy (approximately 20 minutes, €1–2). This is the Istanbul that locals actually inhabit — a dense, lively neighbourhood with an extraordinary market, independent bookshops, street art, and some of the best food in the city.

The Kadıköy market (Salı Pazarı and the surrounding streets) is the most vibrant food market in Istanbul — fish from the Bosphorus and Black Sea, cheeses, olives, vegetables, and street food at prices significantly lower than tourist areas. The Moda neighbourhood adjacent to Kadıköy is the most pleasant walking area in the city.

**Moda promenade:** the coastal path along the southern shore of the Asian peninsula, from Moda's tea gardens to Kalamış marina, is genuinely one of Istanbul's most pleasant walks — particularly at sunset, looking back across the water at the old city.

**Turkish hammam**

A hammam visit is the most distinctly Istanbul experience available, with a 600-year continuous tradition. The process: enter the warm room (ılıklık) to acclimatise, move to the hot room (sıcaklık) for steam, receive a kese (exfoliating scrub with a rough mitten), then a köpük (soap foam massage). The experience lasts 45–90 minutes.

**Recommended hammams for first-timers:**

- **Çemberlitaş Hamamı** (Sultanahmet, open since 1584): €40–80, excellent facilities, tourist-friendly, central location. The most visited for a reason.
- **Süleymaniye Hamamı** (near the Süleymaniye Mosque): €30–50, more local atmosphere, less polished but more authentic.
- **Kılıç Ali Paşa Hamamı** (Karaköy): €80–120, the most beautifully restored hammam in Istanbul — Ottoman architecture preserved at the highest standard. Worth the premium.

Book online to guarantee your time slot and avoid long queues, especially on weekends.

## Where to eat in Istanbul: the honest guide

Turkish cuisine is one of the world's great food traditions, and Istanbul is its highest expression. The city's street food and local restaurants offer exceptional quality at prices that seem implausible compared to Western Europe.

**Street food worth stopping for:**

- **Simit:** sesame-coated bread rings sold from street carts — €0.30–0.50. The city's default breakfast.
- **Balık ekmek (fish sandwich):** grilled mackerel in bread, sold from the boats at Eminönü — €3–4. An Istanbul institution.
- **Döner kebab:** different from the tourist-country version — leaner, on better bread, €3–6 at a local shop.
- **Turkish breakfast (kahvaltı):** multiple small dishes — cheeses, olives, honey, eggs, tomatoes, bread, and unlimited tea — served at breakfast cafés. €5–10/person. The most generous breakfast tradition in Europe.
- **Lahmacun:** thin flatbread with minced meat, herbs, and lemon — €2–3.

**Where not to eat:** the restaurants with menu displays in 12 languages immediately around Hagia Sophia and the Blue Mosque. They are priced 3–5x higher than equivalent quality 200 metres away. Walk into the residential streets and look for places where you see Turkish people eating.

**Where to eat well:**

- **Karaköy:** for coffee, modern Turkish cuisine, and the best meze in the city
- **Kadıköy:** for seafood, local restaurant culture, and the highest value-per-euro ratio in Istanbul
- **Kapalıçarşı (near the bazaar):** Havuzlu Restaurant — one of the oldest in the bazaar, consistent quality for Ottoman-style dishes

## Practical Istanbul: what you need to know

**Transport:** the Istanbulkart (a reusable card, ~€3) covers all public transport — metro, tram, ferry, bus. Single fare: approximately €0.50–1 per trip. The T1 tram runs from Kabataş along the historic peninsula through Sultanahmet to Bağcılar — the most useful line for tourists. Metro M2 connects Taksim to the Grand Bazaar area.

**Getting in from the airport:**
- Istanbul Airport (IST, European side): Havaist bus to Taksim (€10, 45–75 min depending on traffic), or metro to Gayrettepe (€5, 40 min), or taxi (€30–50, use the official metered cabs only)
- Sabiha Gökçen Airport (SAW, Asian side): Havabus to Kadıköy (€10, 30–60 min), or taxi to Asian Istanbul areas (€15–25)

**Money:** withdraw Turkish lira from ATMs (not airport exchange bureaux). Use Revolut or Wise for card payments. Always pay in local currency — refuse DCC. For the complete guide: [how to pay abroad without fees](/en/city-breaks/pay-abroad-no-fees).

**Safety:** Istanbul is safe for tourists. The specific scams to know:
- The **friendly bar invitation** (someone befriends you and takes you to a bar where the bill is €150–400) — decline all unsolicited invitations to go somewhere specific
- **Unlicensed taxis** — use Bolt or the official metered taxis from the airport rank
- **Shoe shine drop** (a shine kit falls near you, then service is expected and charged) — just walk past

**e-Visa:** UK, US, Canadian, Australian, and many other nationals need an e-Visa (evisa.gov.tr, approximately $50–60, takes 5 minutes). Some EU nationalities enter without a visa — check your specific nationality. **Apply before departure.**

**Safety in context:** Turkey's tourist areas are safe. For the full regional safety picture and practical preparation list: [Turkey 2026 safety guide](/en/asigurari/turkey-2026-safety-guide).

**Travel insurance:** mandatory. The EHIC is not valid in Turkey. A single night in a private Istanbul hospital can cost €500–2,000+. Insurance costs €15–25 for a week — see [EHIC vs travel insurance](/en/asigurari/ehic-vs-travel-insurance) for why this matters specifically in Turkey.
