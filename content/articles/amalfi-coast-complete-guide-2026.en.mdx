There are places that make you stop — not because of specific things to see, but because of what the whole scene does to you. The Amalfi Coast is one of them. Standing on a terrace in Ravello at 350 metres above the sea, with the Tyrrhenian stretching to the horizon and lemon groves filling the air below you, triggers something that can't really be described in terms of attractions and itineraries.

It was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1997 — not for any specific monument but for the entire human landscape: the cliffs, the terraced gardens, the architecture, the relationship between villages and sea that has been maintained for centuries. National Geographic has placed it on "must-see in a lifetime" lists. John Steinbeck wrote about it in 1953 with barely suppressed astonishment.

This guide gives you everything needed to actually visit it well in 2026: the truth about transport, the real budget, the hike nobody should skip, what to eat, and the towns that most guides either oversell or completely ignore.

## What the Amalfi Coast Actually Is

The Amalfi Coast (Costiera Amalfitana) is a 50 km stretch of coastline in the Campania region of southern Italy, running from Positano in the west to Vietri sul Mare in the east. It's not one place — it's approximately 13 separate villages clinging to limestone cliffs at various heights, each with its own character, price point, and reason to visit.

The beaches are small and mostly pebbly. This is not a sandy-beach holiday destination in the traditional sense. What it offers instead is extraordinary: clear, warm, turquoise-cobalt water; cliff-diving off limestone formations; snorkelling over ancient amphora fragments; and the most dramatic coastal scenery in Western Europe.

The single road connecting everything — the SS163 Amalfitana, carved into the cliff face in 1853 — has two lanes, is roughly car-width wide, and features tight hairpin bends with drops directly to the sea. Buses and cars negotiate it millimetre by millimetre. This is part of the experience, not a problem to solve, but it shapes every logistical decision you'll make.

## When to Visit — The Honest Seasonal Guide

This might be the single most important decision you make before booking.

### May–June: The Ideal First Visit

Air temperature: 20–26°C. Sea temperature: 19–22°C (comfortable for swimming). Bougainvillea erupts across every façade. Ferries run at full schedule. The coastal towns have atmosphere without the weight of August crowds. Hotel prices are 30–40% below summer peak. The sfusato lemon groves above Minori are in full flower — the fragrance on the Lemon Trail is something you won't forget.

The one consideration: Easter and Italian national holidays (25 April, 1 May) create brief crowding. Avoid these specific dates if possible.

### July–August: Beautiful but Intense

Air temperature: 28–34°C. Sea: 25–28°C — at its best. But: SITA buses frequently depart Amalfi already full, with no room for passengers waiting at intermediate stops. SS163 traffic can double journey times. Hotels at maximum prices. Restaurants without reservations are impossible for dinner. The Feast of the Assumption on 15 August in Positano draws tens of thousands for fireworks and processions — magnificent chaos, but chaos.

The counterintuitive move in July–August: hire a private boat for a morning (€80–150 for a small group) and explore the hidden coves and sea caves from the water, bypassing the land crowds entirely. Or hike early (departure 07:00) when the trails are yours.

### September: The Knowing Traveller's Month

Air temperature: 24–28°C. Sea temperature: 25–27°C — the warmest of the year. Crowds noticeably thinner after the 15th. Prices begin declining. The Ravello Festival concludes in September — the last concerts happen against the backdrop of early autumn light on the sea. The food scene adds harvest dimensions: fresh-pressed Tramonti wine, new-season chestnuts from the mountain villages above.

Local consensus: mid-September to early October is the best window on the entire coast.

### October: Perfect for Hiking and Photography

20–24°C. The light becomes extraordinary — lower angle, warmer tones, sea still swimmable into early October. Some hotels close (check before booking). Ferry schedules reduce. What remains operates beautifully. Harvest festivals in mountain towns above the coast (Scala, Ravello) bring local food at its most seasonal.

### November–March

Most hotels, restaurants, and ferry services close. A few pensioni remain open in Amalfi town and Maiori. Ideal for complete solitude and contemplation; not suitable for a first visit or anyone wanting coastal infrastructure.

## The Towns — What Makes Each One Different

### Positano — The Most Photographed, The Most Expensive

The cascade of pink, yellow, and terracotta houses tumbling down to the sea — this is the image you know. Positano is the most visually dramatic village on the coast and one of the most iconic images in Italy.

It is also the most expensive and, in peak season, the most crowded. A 3-star hotel with sea view: €200–350/night in summer. Le Sirenuse (where John Steinbeck wrote about Positano in 1953, describing it as "a dream place that isn't quite real") starts at €900–1,000/night.

**What the Instagram photos don't show:** Positano is almost entirely stairs. There is almost no flat ground between the parking area (top of town), the village centre (middle), and the beach (bottom). With heavy luggage or limited mobility, it can become exhausting. In summer, the ZTL zone is active 09:00–18:00 — you can only arrive by car before 9am or after 6pm (hotel guests with permission only).

**What's genuinely worth it in Positano:** Spiaggia Grande (the main beach — stunning, though you pay €20–30 for a sunlounger and umbrella). Dinner at Da Vincenzo (the classic local restaurant — lunch is reasonably priced, dinner is a treat). The walk along Via Positanesi d'America on the cliff edge, especially at golden hour.

**The secret:** Fornillo beach, a 10-minute walk west from Spiaggia Grande, is less crowded and more local. And Da Adolfo restaurant, accessible by a free red-striped boat from Positano harbour, serves fresh fish on paper plates with live music for €25–35 per person — one of the best lunch experiences on the entire coast.

### Amalfi Town — The Best Base

Amalfi is the most practical centre of the coast. Ferries to Positano, Salerno, and Sorrento leave from the main harbour. SITA buses stop in the central piazza. Ravello is 20 minutes by bus. It's more affordable than Positano and less isolated than Ravello.

**The Cathedral of Sant'Andrea** (9th–13th century) dominates the main piazza with a façade mixing Arab-Norman and Gothic influences that reflects the commercial reach of the medieval Amalfi Maritime Republic (one of Italy's four great maritime republics alongside Venice, Genoa, and Pisa). Entry: €3. The crypt contains the relics of Saint Andrew — the same Saint Andrew who is the patron of Scotland and Romania.

**Museo della Carta (Paper Museum):** Amalfi produced the first European paper in the 11th century, using technology learned from Arab traders. Water mills on Via delle Cartiere powered the production. Several survive and can be visited. Entry: €5. More interesting than it sounds.

**Atrani:** 5 minutes' walk east from Amalfi, Atrani is the least touristed village on the coast. No large hotels, no car parks, no souvenir shops. A small piazza with a fountain, a church, a few local restaurants, and a small free beach. This is what Positano looked like 30 years ago.

### Ravello — The City of Music, Above the World

Ravello is not on the sea — it's at 350 metres elevation on a plateau above the coast, reached by SITA bus from Amalfi (20 minutes, €1.50). It's cooler, quieter, and has an air quality and light that is unlike anything at sea level.

**Villa Cimbrone:** A villa with 11th-century origins, transformed in the early 20th century by British Lord Grimthorpe. The gardens contain the "Belvedere dell'Infinità" — a terrace with 16 marble busts overlooking the Tyrrhenian Sea in a view that silences most visitors. Greta Garbo and Leopold Stokowski hid from the press at Villa Cimbrone in 1937. Entry: €8.

**Villa Rufolo:** Older (13th century) with terraced gardens built into the clifftop and a concert stage constructed among medieval ruins — the setting for the Ravello Festival each summer (classical music, dance, theatre, June–September). Richard Wagner visited Villa Rufolo in 1880 and found the inspiration for the Klingsor's garden in Parsifal. Entry: €7.

The **Ravello Festival** has a remarkable artistic history: Virginia Woolf, D.H. Lawrence, Gore Vidal (who lived in Ravello for years), and dozens of composers have been drawn by the specific quality of silence and elevation here. If you're visiting during the festival, book concert tickets well in advance.

### Praiano — The Quiet Alternative

Halfway between Positano and Amalfi, Praiano has no ZTL zone and has available parking — making it the most practical base if you're arriving by car. Hotels are 20–30% cheaper than Positano. Marina di Praia — a tiny caletà (fjord-like cove) reached on foot from the village centre — is one of the coast's most beautiful small beaches.

**Sunset direction:** Praiano faces west — which means it catches the Tyrrhenian sunset directly. Every evening, from the village terraces, the light performance over the sea is extraordinary.

### Maiori and Maiori — The Underrated Value

The longest beaches on the coast (Maiori: 300 metres, Minori: 200 metres) and the only stretches where most of the beach is free (no sunlounger required). Prices 30% below Amalfi. Locals from across the coast come to eat in Minori — the culinary reputation is authentic: hand-made pasta with fresh seafood, fresh tuna, simply-cooked fish.

**Roman Villa Maritime Antiquarium in Minori:** A first-century Roman villa discovered beneath the village with mosaics and frescoes demonstrating the region's ancient wealth. Entry: €2.50. Open Tuesday–Sunday mornings.

**The Lemon Trail (Sentiero dei Limoni):** A 3 km path through terraced lemon groves connecting Maiori and Minori — the most fragrant walk on the coast in May when the sfusato amalfitano lemons are flowering.

### Vietri sul Mare — The Ceramics Capital

At the eastern end of the coast near Salerno, Vietri is the source of the hand-painted majolica tiles and pottery you see throughout the Amalfi Coast. Multi-generational ceramic workshops selling handmade pieces at prices well below what you'd pay in Positano boutiques line the streets.

The **Solimene factory** has an extraordinary exterior designed by American architect Paolo Soleri in 1954 — a tiled facade that looks like Gaudí designed a spacecraft. Worth seeing even if you're not buying ceramics.

## Getting Around — The Complete Transport Guide

### SITA Sud Bus (The Budget Backbone)

The only land public transport on the SS163. Operated by SITA Sud (EAV subsidiary). Main routes:
- **Line 5070:** Amalfi–Positano–Sorrento (and return)
- **Line 5120:** Amalfi–Maiori–Salerno (and return)
- **Line 5080:** Amalfi–Agerola–Naples

**Prices:** €1.50–4 per journey depending on distance. 24-hour ticket: ~€10 (check seasonal availability).

**The absolute rule:** buy the ticket BEFORE boarding — at a tabaccheria, bar, or newsagent displaying the SITA logo. It is not sold on the bus. If an inspector boards (it happens), the fine is substantial.

**Timing advice for peak season:** Buses from Sorrento toward Amalfi fill completely at Sorrento in summer. The 09:00 departure is routinely packed before it reaches Positano. Take the 07:30 or wait for 10:30. On the Amalfi–Positano section, buses sometimes arrive from Amalfi already full, leaving passengers waiting at Praiano with no room to board. Allow extra time.

**The view tip:** travelling from Sorrento toward Amalfi, sit on the right side of the bus for sea views. Returning, sit on the left.

**Luggage reality:** SITA buses are small (they navigate SS163's tight bends). Large wheeled suitcases are extremely difficult to manage. Use a backpack or compact bag for coast travel.

### Ferry (The Beautiful Option)

Ferries run April–October connecting Salerno, Amalfi, Positano, Sorrento, Naples, and optionally Capri and Ischia. More expensive than the bus but the experience is on a different level — you see the coast from the water, as every traveller did before the SS163 road was cut into the cliff face in 1853.

**Approximate prices:** Amalfi–Positano: €12–15. Amalfi–Salerno: €8–12. Positano–Sorrento: €18–22. Operators: Travelmar, Alicost, SNAV — check ravello.com/ferry-schedule for current timetables.

**The smart strategy:** take the ferry one direction, the bus the other — combining the sea perspective with the cliff-road perspective.

### Driving Your Own Car — The Full Truth

Driving the Amalfi Coast in season is an experience that can transform a dream holiday into a logistical ordeal. Here's what most guides don't tell you:

**2026 ZTL restrictions:**
- **Positano:** ZTL active 09:00–18:00 in summer (extended to 06:30–midnight in some reports for 2026). Automated cameras register all unauthorised plates. Fine: €80–160, delivered automatically to your home address or rental company. Only residents and hotel guests with specific authorisation can enter.
- **Ravello:** 24/7 ZTL — permanent year-round.
- **Amalfi:** Peak-hour restrictions with camera enforcement.

**The alternating number plate system:** Some days are restricted to even-numbered plates only, others to odd-numbered plates. Motorcycles and scooters are exempt from this rule.

**Parking costs:** €5–10/hour at major locations. Daily parking in Amalfi (private garages): €30–50/day. Positano's private garages (at the top of town) fill by 10:00 in summer.

**The intelligent car strategy:** Park at Salerno (Piazza della Concordia, ~€5/day) or Sorrento (Garage Achille Lauro, ~€2/hour) and use ferry or SITA bus from there. Praiano has parking and no ZTL — if you want a car-based base, Praiano is it.

**Private driver with NCC licence:** A licensed driver (Noleggio con Conducente) has permanent ZTL access to all towns — they drop you at your hotel door without fines. Cost: €200–300 for a full-day coast tour from Naples, €120–180 for a transfer to Positano. Expensive, but eliminates all logistical stress.

## The Path of the Gods (Sentiero degli Dei) — Complete Hiking Guide

This is the most spectacular hiking trail in southern Italy. Arguably in Italy.

**The classic route:** Bomerano (Agerola, 618 m elevation) to Nocelle (250 m elevation). Distance: 7.8 km. Duration: 3–4 hours walking, 4–5 hours with stops. Predominantly downhill.

**Why Bomerano to Nocelle (not the reverse):** Walking downhill with the panorama in front of you — the sea, Positano, the Li Galli archipelago (the three islands that ancient tradition identifies as the site of the Sirens in Homer's Odyssey) appear ahead of you as you descend. The reverse route climbs 500 m of altitude with the view behind you and the sun in your face.

**The trail name:** "Path of the Gods" derives from local tradition that the mountain ridge was so high above the human world that only gods could walk there — and from the specific difficulty (narrow limestone path with drop-offs) that made it inaccessible to casual walkers before modern trail maintenance.

**How to get to Bomerano:** SITA bus Line 5080 from Amalfi toward Agerola (45 min, ~€2). Or taxi from Amalfi (~€25–30). Or organised tour with transport included via GetYourGuide.

**From Nocelle to Positano:** 1,700 steps downhill on foot (45 minutes, spectacular but demanding on the knees), or local bus Nocelle–Positano (~15 minutes). From Positano, ferry or SITA bus back to Amalfi.

**Mandatory booking July–August:** approximately €5, via the Parco dei Monti Lattari portal. Rest of the year: free access.

**What to bring:** grippy sole footwear (the limestone path is dusty and can be slippery in damp conditions), minimum 1.5L water (no sources on the trail), SPF50 sunscreen, hat, energy snacks. There is no shade on the main trail section.

**Best time of day:** start 07:00–08:00 — you avoid midday heat and the organised tour groups that depart later. In August, by 10:00 the trail is significantly crowded and hot.

**The high variant (Sentiero degli Dei Alto):** adds approximately 2 km and 1 hour, traversing the higher ridges of Monte Peruso. Views are even broader; almost no other hikers.

### Other Hiking Trails

**Valle delle Ferriere (from Amalfi town):** 5 km round-trip through a ravine with ruins of medieval paper mills, the only naturally occurring papyrus forest in continental Europe, and a small waterfall at the end. 2 hours, moderate difficulty. Completely different landscape from the coastal hikes.

**Sentiero dei Limoni (Maiori–Minori):** 3 km through terraced lemon groves connecting the two towns. 2 hours, easy. The most fragrant trail on the coast in May.

**Bomerano–Santa Maria del Castello–Conca dei Marini loop:** A less-hiked 10 km loop from the Agerola plateau giving a different perspective on the coast than the Sentiero degli Dei. 4–5 hours.

## The Food — What to Eat and Where

### The Sfusato Amalfitano Lemon

This is the fundamental ingredient of the entire coast's food culture. Larger than standard lemons, spindle-shaped, with a thick, intensely perfumed peel that is so sweet it can be sliced and eaten in salads or with just olive oil. The word *sfusato* comes from the Italian for "spindle," referencing the elongated shape.

The lemons grow on terraced "giardini di limoni" — vertical garden plots carved into the cliff faces, supported by chestnut poles and shade nets, forming shaded green tunnels that you walk under when visiting the lemon groves above Amalfi and Minori. Many local producers offer farm visits and limoncello tastings.

**Limoncello made from sfusato amalfitano** is categorically different from commercial varieties. Serve it from the freezer in frosted glasses. Buy from local producers in Ravello or from the terraced groves above Amalfi — not from souvenir shops at the port.

**Delizia al limone:** A dome-shaped sponge cake soaked in limoncello syrup and filled with lemon cream — the coast's signature pastry. Found at every pasticceria and café. Get one.

### The Dishes You Need to Know

**Scialatielli ai frutti di mare:** Scialatielli is a pasta specific to the Amalfi Coast — thicker, shorter, with a rougher texture that holds sauce better than standard pasta. Served with clams, mussels, squid, octopus, and the day's fresh catch. This dish, as made here, cannot be replicated elsewhere because the seafood was swimming hours ago.

**Spaghetti alle vongole:** Pasta with small clams (vongole veraci), olive oil, garlic, white wine, parsley. Appears simple; tastes profound. On the Amalfi Coast, the clams are hours old at most — the difference from a restaurant in London or Vienna is categorical.

**Totani e patate:** Squid and potato, slow-cooked together with tomatoes, garlic, and white wine. If you find this in a restaurant with no English menu, a visible stovetop, and no photos of dishes on the walls — order it.

**Pasta alla Nerano:** Invented at Lo Scoglio restaurant in Marina del Cantone (near Positano) — pasta with fried zucchini, provolone del Monaco cheese, and basil. Simple ingredients, extraordinary result. The original version is only available at Lo Scoglio.

**Fritto misto:** Mixed small fish and seafood, fried. At the sea, fresh, with a wedge of sfusato lemon. Not street food — the ritual lunch of locals.

**Mozzarella di bufala:** From inland Campania, served everywhere fresh. A different product from the supermarket version — soft as a cloud, milk-flavoured, still warm in some cases.

### Local Wines

**Fiano di Avellino** and **Greco di Tufo** (Campania white wines) pair perfectly with the coast's seafood. **Taurasi** is the serious red. But the most interesting are the **Tramonti and Furore wines** — produced in tiny quantities from vines grown on the coast's terraces. You won't find them in commercial distribution; you drink them at local restaurants or buy directly from producers.

### Where to Eat — Real Recommendations

**Trattoria da Memè (Amalfi town):** Lunch €15, menu in Italian only, locals at every table. Exactly what you're looking for.

**Lo Scoglio (Marina del Cantone, Nerano):** 30 minutes from Positano over the hill. On stilts above the sea. Fresh seafood of extraordinary quality. The birthplace of pasta alla Nerano. Advance booking essential.

**Da Adolfo (Spiaggia di Laurito, Positano):** The free red-striped boat from Positano harbour takes you there. Paper tablecloths, grilled fish with lemon, live music. €25–35/person for a complete meal — one of the best lunch experiences on the coast.

**Ristorante La Tagliata (above Positano):** Fixed family menu (€40) — hand-made pasta, grilled meat, local vegetables, desserts. Views over Positano and the sea. Find it via a narrow road climbing out of Positano.

**Gelato at any local gelateria in Amalfi:** Lemon gelato made with sfusato — get this before you leave. The difference from standard lemon sorbet is significant.

## Day Trips from the Amalfi Coast

### Capri — Half-Day by Ferry

Ferry from Positano or Amalfi: ~40–50 minutes, €20–30 one-way. **Grotta Azzurra (Blue Grotto):** extraordinary optical phenomenon — sunlight entering below the cave walls through water illuminates everything in electric blue. Visited by small rowboat (€14 boat + €15 entry). Faraglioni — three vertical limestone sea stacks at the island's southern tip. Anacapri (upper town) is significantly quieter than Capri town.

**Budget note:** Capri is expensive. A basic lunch: €30–40/person. Capri is not the place for a casual coffee; it's for a special meal or the specific Blue Grotto experience.

### Pompeii and Herculaneum

1–1.5 hours from the coast by train plus bus or car. Pompeii: vast, overwhelming, impossible to cover in under 4 hours. Herculaneum: smaller, better preserved, more intimate — wooden structures survived, frescoes are intact, everyday objects remain that Pompeii no longer has. Pompeii entry: €18. Herculaneum entry: €13. For a single day trip, Herculaneum is actually more rewarding for first-time visitors.

### Naples

1–2 hours from the coast. The Museo Nazionale Napolitano contains the world's most important collection of Roman antiquities — the Pompeii frescoes, mosaics, and objects transferred for preservation. Spaccanapoli, the long straight street that bisects the historic centre. Pizzeria da Michele (founded 1870, €5 for the most famous pizza in the world). Naples is intense, chaotic, and genuinely extraordinary — a full day.

## Practical Tips — What Most Guides Don't Tell You

**Book accommodation 3–4 months ahead** for July–August. The best positions in Amalfi and Ravello sell out by March–April. For September, 4–6 weeks ahead is usually sufficient.

**Don't rent a car if you're staying on the coast** — you'll use it 20% of the time and pay for parking the other 80%, or receive ZTL fines. Rent a car specifically for excursions to Pompeii, Naples, or the Cilento coast, not for coast-to-coast movement.

**Coperto** (cover charge, €2–4/person) is legal and traditional in Italy. It's not a scam. Check the bill before paying, but don't become irritated about it.

**Reserve the best restaurants 2–3 days ahead** in July–August. Lo Scoglio, Da Adolfo, and La Tagliata fill their limited seating quickly.

**Photography timing:** early morning (07:00–09:00) — soft light, almost no tourists. Late afternoon (17:00–19:00) — golden hour. Avoid midday (12:00–14:00) — harsh light and maximum crowding.

**Italian SIM and data:** EU free roaming applies for EU visitors. If your plan has low data limits, an Italian eSIM from [Airalo](https://airalo.tpo.mx/mYekFAo3) covers the gap from €4–5.

**Travel insurance:** Italy is EU, so your EHIC card works at public hospitals. A full travel insurance policy (from [EKTA](https://ektatraveling.tpo.mx/uU7jZ5jK), ~€15/week) adds repatriation, trip cancellation, and baggage cover — worth it for a trip of this value.

**Climbing the Amalfi Coast by scooter** is exempt from ZTL and number-plate restrictions — if you're an experienced rider (the SS163 is genuinely demanding), renting a scooter from Agerola gives you access everywhere, every day.

## How to Get There

**By air:** Fly to Naples International Airport (NAP). From Bucharest, Wizz Air and Ryanair operate direct flights from €45–90 return.

**From Naples Airport to the Coast:**
- **To Sorrento (western coast gateway):** EAV shuttle bus Naples Airport → Sorrento direct (~70 min, €10). Or train (to Naples Garibaldi, then Circumvesuviana to Sorrento, €4 — works, but luggage through Naples metro is tiring).
- **To Salerno (eastern coast gateway):** Regional train from Naples Airport to Naples Centrale, then Frecciarossa/Intercity to Salerno (€12–20, 40 min). From Salerno, SITA bus or ferry.
- **Private transfer direct to Positano or Amalfi:** €150–200 per car, eliminates all transit complications.

**From Rome:** High-speed train Rome Termini → Naples Centrale (1h10, €25–50). Then as above.

**From elsewhere in Europe:** Connections via Rome, Milan, or direct low-cost services into Naples.
