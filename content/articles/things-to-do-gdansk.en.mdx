Gdańsk is the city where the most destructive century in European history began — and where the largest ideological empire of the 20th century started to fall apart. On September 1, 1939, the first shot of World War II was fired at Westerplatte, the peninsula at the entrance to Gdańsk harbour. Forty-one years later, in August 1980, at the Lenin Shipyard in the same city, an electrician named Lech Wałęsa climbed a fence and launched the strike that created Solidarność — the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, which triggered in cascade the fall of Communist governments across Eastern Europe.

Between these two historical moments, Gdańsk survived, was destroyed 90% in 1945, and was methodically rebuilt brick by brick following the original plans preserved in the city's archives.

What emerged from the reconstruction is remarkable: a Long Street (Długi Targ) of coloured Gothic townhouses, with ornate facades and intact medieval cellars — more beautiful, paradoxically, than the original in pre-1945 photographs.

## Długi Targ and the Long Street — the Rebuilt Heart

The main axis of Gdańsk is the Royal Way: the Long Street (Długa) opening into the Long Market (Długi Targ), flanked by Gothic and Renaissance bourgeois houses in yellow, red, green, and white, each with a different facade, with cellars now housing restaurants, bars, and shops.

**Neptune's Fountain** — from 1633, in the center of Długi Targ, the city's unofficial symbol. Free, photographable at any hour.

**Artus Court (Dwór Artusa)** — the medieval merchants' banqueting hall, with the most impressive ceramic stove in Europe (10.6m tall, 520 different tiles). Part of the Gdańsk Museum network.

**Old Town Hall** — the 83-meter tower, with a panoramic view from the top.

**Golden Gate and Green Gate** — carved stone portals marking the entrances to the Royal Way.

## Mariacka Street — the World's Amber

If there is one street in Gdańsk to visit with precision, it is Mariacką — a narrow medieval alley, with stone steps at each house entrance, ornate balconies, and dozens of amber shops and workshops that have extended their displays onto the pavement.

The Polish Baltic coast produces **70–90% of the world's amber** — fossilized tree resin 44 million years old, sometimes with insects or plants perfectly preserved inside. Gdańsk has been the center of the amber trade (called "the gold of the north" or Bernstein in German) for at least 3,000 years.

**What to buy:** simple jewelry (earrings, bracelets) from €10–30, necklaces €30–100, artistic pieces with inclusions €100–500+. Amber with insects inside is far rarer and more valuable.

**How not to be deceived:** authentic amber is lightweight — if it's heavy, it's glass or plastic. Simple test: rub the piece quickly between your fingers — authentic amber smells faintly of pine resin. Buy from shops with authenticity certificates issued by the Polish Amber Association.

**Amber Museum** (in the medieval Great Mill, 5 minutes from Mariacką): exhibition of spectacular inclusion amber, the history of the trade, and contemporary artistic pieces. Entry: ~€7.

## St. Mary's Basilica — the World's Largest Brick Church

Built between 1343–1502, St. Mary's Basilica is the **world's largest brick church** — it can hold 25,000 people. 27 lateral chapels, a 15th-century astronomical clock, and enormous Gothic windows.

**The tower** — 400+ steps, complete panorama of Gdańsk and the harbour. **Price: ~€2.**

**Basilica entry:** ~€5 adults (combined discount with tower available).

**Tip:** The medieval astronomical clock inside shows the time, date, and lunar phases — built in 1464 by Hans Düringer, it is one of the rare functioning medieval clocks in Europe.

## Museum of the Second World War — Europe's Best War Museum

Opened in 2017 after a 10-year political saga, the Museum of the Second World War is considered by many historians to be the most complete and thoughtfully designed WWII museum in the world. Unlike national museums that present the war from a single country's perspective, this one presents the global conflict — with equal sections dedicated to the European, Pacific, and African theatres, with special attention to civilian populations.

**2,000+ artefacts:** a Sherman tank, a full-scale reconstructed interwar Warsaw street, the Holocaust section, Nazi and Soviet propaganda, and a perspective that avoids no part of the truth — including Red Army crimes in Poland.

## European Solidarity Centre — Where Communism Fell

The European Solidarity Centre is built **on the exact site** of the Lenin Shipyard where Lech Wałęsa organised the August 1980 strike. The building itself is a work of art: Corten steel that mimics rust — the colour of iron and industrial labour — with the silhouette of a cargo vessel seen from above.

Inside: the permanent exhibition about Solidarność and Eastern European resistance movements is, simply, one of the most emotionally powerful museum experiences in Europe. Original posters, secret documents of the Polish security service (SB), audio recordings of the 1980 negotiations, and testimonies from activists. The section on Solidarity's chain-reaction effect — from Poland to Hungary, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Romania — contextualises a moment that younger generations know only from textbooks.

**Prices:** ~34 PLN (~€8) adults.

**Personal note:** If you grew up under Communism or have parents who lived through it, this exhibition has a level of personal resonance that transcends any guidebook. Allow 2 hours.

## Westerplatte — Where World War II Began

The Westerplatte peninsula, 7 km from central Gdańsk, is where on September 1, 1939, at 4:47 AM, the German battleship Schleswig-Holstein fired the first shot of World War II at a Polish garrison of 182 soldiers.

The garrison held for 7 days — against Germany's estimate of a few hours. All 182 defenders are treated as national heroes in Poland.

**How to get there:** Ferry from central Gdańsk (summer, ~40 min, €10–15 return) or bus (included in transport pass, 40 min). Alternatively, an organised tour that includes context and a guide.

**Entry to the monument:** Free. Small museum on site: ~€3.

## The Crane, Motława Waterfront and Żuraw

**Żuraw** (The Crane) is the symbol of Gdańsk's port — a medieval wooden crane from 1444, the largest and best-preserved medieval crane in Europe, now part of the National Maritime Museum.

**Prices:** ~€6 adults (includes waterfront access and interior).

The Motława riverbank is the most photographed spot in Gdańsk — the coloured houses on the opposite bank (Ołowianka island), boats, waterfront restaurant terraces. In the evening, with illumination reflected in the water, it is spectacular.

## Sopot — Europe's Longest Pier

15 minutes by SKM train (~€1.50), Sopot is completely different from Gdańsk — an elegant resort town with Art Nouveau architecture, fine sand beaches, and **Monte Cassino** — Poland's most animated pedestrian boulevard, with restaurants, bars, and shops along 450 meters.

**Molo (Pier):** Europe's longest wooden pier — 511 meters over the Baltic Sea. From the end, on clear days, you can see the coastline stretching in both directions.

| Detail | Info |
|---|---|
| Pier length | 511 meters |
| Entry ticket | ~€2 adults |
| Sopot beach | Free |
| Gdańsk–Sopot train | ~€1.50 / 15 minutes |

**Beach season:** The Baltic Sea warms to 16–20°C in July–August — cold compared to the Mediterranean, but perfect for Poles and those accustomed to northern seas. The beaches are clean and well-equipped.

**Tip:** Sopot is perfect for the final evening — dinner with fresh Baltic fish on Monte Cassino, followed by a walk on the pier at sunset, before the train back to Gdańsk.

## Malbork Castle — The World's Largest Castle

45 minutes by train from Gdańsk (~€2 return), Malbork Castle is the **world's largest castle by area** and the most impressive Gothic architectural complex in Central Europe. Built by the Teutonic Knights from red brick between the 13th–15th centuries, UNESCO since 1997.

The castle has three concentric fortresses — the High Castle, the Middle Castle, and the Lower Castle — with walls 3–4 meters thick, towers, drawbridges, and a chapel with 14th-century Gothic stained glass restored after World War II.

**Prices:**

## Food in Gdańsk

Gdańsk cuisine is Polish cooking with a strong emphasis on **Baltic fish** and port flavours: herring, cod, smoked salmon, Baltic shrimp.

### The Essential Dishes

**Herring in cream sauce (Śledź w śmietanie)** — Gdańsk's port specialty, served with pickles and dark bread. €4–7.

**Żurek** — Polish sour rye soup, identical to what you find in Kraków. €5–8.

**Pierogi** — Polish dumplings in all varieties. €6–9 a portion.

**Kotlet schabowy** — breaded pork cutlet, Poland's national dish, filling and cheap. €6–10.

**Goldwasser** — the local liqueur, with real 24-karat gold flakes in suspension, produced in Gdańsk since 1598. Drunk as a digestif or in cocktails. €4–7 a glass. Leave Gdańsk without drinking a Goldwasser and you haven't really been there.

**Dark bread with lard and pickles (Chleb ze smalcem)** — the port snack, €2–3, found in every traditional pub.

**Pączki** — Polish doughnuts filled with rose jam or cream, far better than any Western equivalent. €1–2 each.

### Where to Eat Cheaply

**Bar Mleczny Neptun** (Długi Targ) — the Communist-era milk bar in the heart of the most tourist area in Gdańsk. Full lunch under €5. One of the rare cases where a milk bar sits right in the tourist center.

**Pierogarnia Mandu** — specialised exclusively in pierogi, with creative options alongside the classics.

**Hala Targowa** — the covered market in the center, cheap local food stalls.

## When to Visit Gdańsk

| Period | Temperatures | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| December–February | −5–5°C | Famous Christmas markets, minimum prices | Cold, Baltic wind, short days |
| March–May | 8–18°C | **Excellent**: less crowded, good prices | Sea cold (10–14°C) |
| June | 18–23°C | Pleasantly warm, beaches opening | Growing crowds |
| July–August | 22–27°C | **Peak**: beaches, festivals, St. Dominic's Fair | Most crowded, maximum prices |
| September–October | 14–20°C | **Ideal**: smaller crowds, good prices, sea OK | Cool evenings |
| November | 5–10°C | Quiet, low prices | Grey and rainy |

**St. Dominic's Fair** (August) — Poland's oldest fair, with 750+ years of history, transforms Gdańsk for 3 weeks into a carnival of crafts, music, and gastronomy. Hotel prices double during this period.

**Gdańsk Christmas Markets** are considered among Poland's most beautiful — Długi Targ transformed into a winter village with amber, crafts, and mulled wine.

## Insider Tips — What Locals Know

**1. Avoid Euronet ATMs** (yellow + blue). They charge hidden fees — €4–5 per withdrawal. Use Polish bank ATMs (PKO, Pekao, ING) or pay directly by card.

**2. Authentic vs tourist Goldwasser.** Goldwasser liqueur with real 24-karat gold flakes has been produced since 1598 — buy from specialist shops or old restaurants, not from tourist stalls at the airport (where it's a diluted version at 3× the price).

**3. The SKM train beats taxis across Trójmiasto.** Gdańsk–Sopot in 15 minutes for €1.50 vs taxi 20–30 minutes for €15–20. No competition.

**4. The Baltic Sea is cold even in summer.** 16–20°C in August — perfect for Poles, surprising for Mediterranean visitors. Bring a swimsuit, but don't rely on Greek-temperature water.

**5. Malbork Castle has queues in summer.** Book tickets online at zamek.malbork.pl or through GetYourGuide. Arrive at the first entry (9:00 AM) and avoid the coach tour groups that arrive at 10:30–11:00.

**6. St. Dominic's Fair triples prices.** If you're going in August, check whether your dates coincide with the Fair — 3 weeks of festival that brings millions of visitors and fills every hotel.

**7. Amber with insects is 10–100× rarer.** Pieces with perfectly preserved insects or plants are exponentially more valuable than plain amber. The price reflects the rarity — if it seems too cheap, the inclusion is artificial or the material is fake.
