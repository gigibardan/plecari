You are sitting at the gate, boarding pass ready, and the departures board changes: DELAYED. Or worse — CANCELLED. The airline desk has a queue of 50 people. Nobody is volunteering information. What most passengers do not realise at this moment is that they may have a legal right to €250–€600 in cash compensation — in addition to whatever the airline arranges. Here is exactly how it works.

## The legal framework: EU Regulation 261/2004

EU Regulation 261/2004 (usually called "EU261") is the European law that protects air passengers. It has been in force since 2005 and has been progressively strengthened by European Court of Justice rulings over the years.

**It applies to:**
- All flights **departing from any EU/EEA airport** — regardless of which airline operates the flight
- All flights **arriving at an EU/EEA airport** operated by an EU-based airline
- Flights from and to Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein (EEA members)
- Flights from UK airports (under UK261, the near-identical post-Brexit equivalent)
- Flights from Switzerland (bilateral agreement)

**Practical examples:**
- Wizz Air Bucharest → London: ✅ covered (departs EU)
- Ryanair Dublin → Barcelona: ✅ covered (departs EU, EU carrier)
- Turkish Airlines Istanbul → Vienna: ✅ covered (arrives EU, but only if operated by an EU carrier — check carefully)
- Delta Air Lines New York → Amsterdam: ❌ not covered by EU261 (departs non-EU, non-EU carrier)
- British Airways London → New York: ✅ covered by UK261 (departs UK)

**2026 update:** The European Parliament voted in January 2026 on proposed amendments to EU261, currently in the legislative process. Proposed changes include clearer definitions of extraordinary circumstances and updated distance thresholds. Until final adoption, the 2004 regulation and its Court of Justice interpretations remain in force.

## When you are entitled to compensation

Compensation is triggered by three distinct situations:

### 1. Flight delay of 3+ hours at your final destination

The clock runs on **arrival time at your final destination**, not departure time. A flight that departs 4 hours late but makes up 1 hour en route counts as a 3-hour delay — triggering full compensation.

**Critical point:** if you have a connection, what matters is when you arrive at your **final destination**. Missing a connection due to a delayed first flight and arriving 4 hours late at your destination qualifies, even if the first flight was only delayed 45 minutes.

### 2. Flight cancellation with less than 14 days' notice

If the airline cancels your flight and notifies you:
- **Less than 7 days before departure:** full compensation (€250/400/600) unless an alternative flight is offered within very tight time windows
- **7–14 days before departure:** compensation unless an alternative flight is offered within specific windows
- **More than 14 days before departure:** no compensation (but you are entitled to a refund of the full ticket price)

**Important:** if the airline offers an alternative flight that gets you to your destination within 2 hours of the original arrival time (for flights under 1,500 km) or within 3–4 hours (for longer flights), they can reduce the compensation by 50%. If the alternative they offer is worse than this, full compensation applies.

### 3. Denied boarding due to overbooking

Airlines routinely sell more seats than are available, expecting some passengers not to show up. When everyone does show up and the airline asks for volunteers — then involuntarily denies boarding — EU261 compensation applies in full, in addition to the alternative flight or refund.

**If you volunteer:** you negotiate your own compensation with the airline in exchange for voluntarily giving up your seat. This can sometimes be more generous than the legal minimum — but get it in writing before giving up your boarding pass.

## How much compensation you receive

| Flight distance | Delay trigger | Compensation |
|---|---|---|
| Under 1,500 km | 3+ hours | **€250** |
| 1,500–3,500 km (intra-EU over 1,500 km) | 3+ hours | **€400** |
| 1,500–3,500 km (non-EU) | 4+ hours | **€400** |
| Over 3,500 km | 4+ hours | **€600** |

**The amount does not depend on the ticket price.** A €29 Ryanair ticket and a €290 business fare on the same disrupted route generate identical compensation.

**For a family of four** on a cancelled London–Athens flight (approximately 2,400 km): 4 × €400 = **€1,600 total**.

**Reduction by 50%** is allowed if the airline provides an alternative flight arriving within: 2 hours of original (under 1,500 km), 3 hours (1,500–3,500 km), or 4 hours (over 3,500 km).

## Extraordinary circumstances: the airline's main defence

Airlines are not required to pay compensation if they can prove the disruption was caused by "extraordinary circumstances" that could not have been avoided even with all reasonable measures.

### What qualifies as extraordinary circumstances

**Weather:** storms, lightning strikes, volcanic ash, dense fog. Not all bad weather qualifies — it must be genuinely severe and specifically affecting your flight's operation. Light rain causing a delay does not qualify.

**Air traffic control (ATC) strikes:** if ATC staff at the destination or en-route country go on strike, disrupting the airspace. This is outside the airline's control.

**Security incidents:** confirmed security threats, bomb scares, or airport security breaches that force evacuations or groundings.

**Medical emergencies on board:** if a flight is diverted because a passenger requires emergency medical attention, causing delays to the subsequent rotation.

**Airport or runway closures:** confirmed closures by airport authority for reasons outside the airline's control.

### What does NOT qualify as extraordinary circumstances

**Technical faults:** this is the most frequently abused claim by airlines. The European Court of Justice confirmed (Wallentin-Hermann v. Alitalia, 2008) that technical malfunctions are part of normal airline operations and do not constitute extraordinary circumstances. If your flight is delayed because of a mechanical fault, the airline owes compensation.

**Airline staff strikes:** strikes by the airline's own pilots, cabin crew, or ground staff are within the airline's sphere of responsibility and do not qualify as extraordinary circumstances (Krüsemann and Others v. TUIfly, European Court of Justice, 2018).

**Knock-on effects from earlier disruptions:** if a flight is delayed because the incoming aircraft was late due to an extraordinary circumstance affecting an earlier flight, the airline sometimes attempts to use this as a defence. Courts have been mixed on this — it depends on whether the airline had alternative aircraft available.

**Crew duty time limits:** if a delay causes the crew to exceed their legally permitted working hours, requiring a replacement crew, this is generally not an extraordinary circumstance.

## What the airline must provide — beyond compensation

EU261 requires two separate things: **care** (right of assistance) and **compensation** (cash payment). These are independent — you can be entitled to care without compensation (e.g. if extraordinary circumstances apply) and entitled to compensation regardless of whether care was provided.

### Right of care (assistance)

Regardless of the cause of the disruption:

**Delay of 2+ hours (short flights under 1,500 km):**
- Meals and refreshments in reasonable relation to the waiting time
- Two telephone calls, emails, or faxes

**Delay of 3+ hours (medium and long flights):**
- Meals and refreshments
- Two telephone calls, emails, or faxes

**Delay causing an overnight stay:**
- Hotel accommodation
- Transport between the airport and hotel

**Practical implication:** if the airline does not proactively provide meals, drinks, and hotel (if applicable), you can purchase these yourself and claim reimbursement. Keep all receipts. The reimbursement is separate from and in addition to the cash compensation.

### Right to reimbursement or re-routing

If the delay at your departure airport exceeds 5 hours, you have the additional right to:
- A full refund of your ticket (all unused portions) within 7 days
- Plus, if your journey is split and you are already mid-trip, a return flight to your original departure point

This means: if your connecting flight is delayed for 5 hours and you decide you would rather go home than continue, you can request a full refund and a free flight home.

## What to do at the airport — step by step

Disruptions are stressful. Knowing exactly what to do before it happens means you collect the right evidence and protect your right to claim.

**Step 1: Document everything immediately**
- Photograph the departures board showing your flight number and the delay/cancellation notice
- Note the exact time the delay was announced and the stated reason (if any)
- Screenshot any notifications received from the airline

**Step 2: Get the reason in writing**
Ask the airline desk for a written statement of the reason for the delay or cancellation. Airline staff are often unwilling to provide this — if they refuse, note that refusal in writing yourself and email it to yourself with a timestamp.

**Step 3: Collect your care entitlements**
Request meal vouchers or reimbursement for food and drinks. If the airline does not provide them, buy your own and keep the receipts (reasonable amounts — a restaurant meal, not a bottle of champagne).

**Step 4: Accept the alternative flight if offered — but do not waive compensation**
If you accept an alternative flight, note: accepting the re-routing does NOT waive your right to financial compensation under EU261. These are separate entitlements. Do not sign any document that links accepting the alternative flight to forfeiting compensation, unless you have read it carefully and are satisfied with the terms.

**Step 5: Do not accept a voucher under pressure**
Airlines sometimes offer travel vouchers at the gate or soon after. You are not obliged to accept a voucher instead of cash compensation. If you accept it voluntarily knowing it replaces your cash right, that is your choice. If you accepted it believing you had no alternative, you may still be able to pursue the cash amount.

**Step 6: Record your actual arrival time**
Your compensation eligibility depends on when you arrive at your final destination, not when you depart. Note or screenshot the actual arrival time (when the aircraft doors open, not when you land).

## How to claim — two approaches

### Option 1: Claim directly with the airline (free, you keep 100%)

Most airlines have an online claims form or a dedicated customer service contact for EU261 claims. The process:

1. Go to the airline's website and find the "compensation" or "claims" section
2. Submit: flight number, date, original and actual arrival times, your contact details and bank information
3. The airline has 2–6 weeks to respond (legally they should respond, though many delay)
4. If they refuse or do not respond within 6 weeks, escalate to the national enforcement body

**National enforcement bodies:**
- UK: Civil Aviation Authority (caa.co.uk)
- Ireland: Commission for Aviation Regulation
- Germany: Luftfahrt-Bundesamt
- France: DGAC (Direction Générale de l'Aviation Civile)
- Other EU countries: each has a national aviation authority that handles EU261 complaints

**Success rate:** airlines approve many straightforward claims directly. For clear-cut cases (mechanical delay confirmed by the airline, cancellation with short notice), direct claims have a reasonable success rate. Airlines become less cooperative for borderline cases or when they plan to invoke extraordinary circumstances.

## Special scenarios

### Missed connections

If you have a single booking (one PNR/reservation reference) with a connection, and a delay on the first flight causes you to miss the connection:

- Compensation is calculated on the **total journey distance** (departure to final destination)
- The **arrival time that matters** is when you reach your final destination
- The airline must re-route you to your destination as quickly as possible

**Example:** Bucharest → Vienna → New York on a single Lufthansa booking. The Bucharest–Vienna leg is delayed, causing you to miss the Vienna–New York connection. You arrive in New York 5 hours late. Compensation: €600 (journey over 3,500 km), not €250 (just the Bucharest–Vienna segment).

**Two separate bookings:** if you booked Bucharest → Vienna on one booking and Vienna → New York on another, and you miss the connection, you are entitled to compensation for the first flight's delay (if it qualifies) but the airline has no obligation regarding the second booking.

### Long delays at the destination — denied return flight

If your return flight (the one bringing you home) is cancelled or significantly delayed, EU261 applies in exactly the same way as for outbound flights, subject to the same distance rules.
