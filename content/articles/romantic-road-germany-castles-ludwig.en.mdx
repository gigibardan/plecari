There is a road in Bavaria that gave the world the Disney castle. Not metaphorically — <cite index="111-1">Neuschwanstein, the castle of King Ludwig II of Bavaria, directly inspired the design of the Sleeping Beauty Castle at Disneyland.</cite> And it stands at the end of a 460 km route that passes through some of the best-preserved medieval towns in Europe.

The Romantische Straße — Romantic Road — was created in 1950 as a tourist route connecting Würzburg to Füssen, following a former Roman trade road. <cite index="116-1">It is Germany's most popular holiday route</cite> and attracts over 2 million visitors annually. It is not popular because it is artificial — it is popular because it concentrates on 460 km more intact medieval architecture, more castles, and more historical depth than any comparable route in Central Europe.

And at its southern end, in the Bavarian Alps, stand the palaces of a king who lived more in his dreams than in reality. <cite index="124-1">"I want to remain an eternal mystery to myself and others," Ludwig once told his governess.</cite> He succeeded — 140 years later, nobody knows with certainty how he died.

---

## King Ludwig II — the Mad King Who Built Fairy Tales

Before talking about castles, you need to understand the man who built them. <cite index="124-1">Ludwig II was born on August 25, 1845 at Schloss Nymphenburg, became King of Bavaria in 1864, and died on June 13, 1886 in Lake Starnberg, at age 40.</cite>

He was a radical introvert in an era that demanded monarchs be public and political. Shy, obsessed with Richard Wagner's operas and Germanic-Nordic medieval mythology, Ludwig was not interested in power or ceremony. He was interested in architecture. In music. In solitude.

He ruled Bavaria for 22 years without causing a single war or major economic crisis. He spent his entire personal fortune — not public funds — building three impossible castles in the Alpine mountains. <cite index="128-1">Linderhof was where Ludwig actually lived in his last years, taking solitary walks at night through the illuminated gardens.</cite>

In 1886, a council of psychiatrists declared him insane and unfit to rule, without having ever personally examined him. The day after his arrest, his body was found in Lake Starnberg, alongside the doctor accompanying him. Circumstances remain unexplained. Political assassination? Suicide? Accident? <cite index="124-1">His palaces, which were barred to strangers during his lifetime, have been visited by over 130 million people since his death.</cite>

<cite index="125-1">In 2025, UNESCO inscribed Ludwig II's palaces — Neuschwanstein, Linderhof, Herrenchiemsee, and the King's House on Schachen — as a serial World Heritage Site</cite>, recognizing "the romantic and eclectic spirit of the era" and their integration into the Bavarian Alpine landscape.

## The Full Route — North to South

The Romantic Road begins in **Würzburg** (northern Bavaria, 2h30 from Frankfurt) and ends in **Füssen** (southern Bavaria, 2h from Munich, at the Austrian border). It covers 460 km through Franconia and Bavaria, passing through medieval towns, plains, and finally Alpine mountains.

**Recommended direction:** north to south, Würzburg → Füssen. The scenery improves progressively, and the Alpine finale is the climax — better to finish with Neuschwanstein than to see it first.

## Würzburg and the Bishop's Palace — the Baroque Start

**Würzburg** is more than a starting point. <cite index="116-1">The Romantic Road begins in Würzburg with the magnificent Residenz palace — a Baroque masterpiece by Balthasar Neumann and one of many German UNESCO World Heritage sites.</cite>

**Würzburg Residenz** — a Bishop's palace built 1720–1744 with the world's largest ceiling fresco, painted by Venetian artist Giovanni Battista Tiepolo. The fresco in the Imperial Hall (Kaisersaal) covers the entire vaulted ceiling and depicts Frederick Barbarossa surrounded by allegorical figures of the continents.

## Rothenburg ob der Tauber — Europe's Most Intact Medieval Town

<cite index="121-1">Rothenburg ob der Tauber is the jewel of the Romantic Road — one of Europe's best-preserved medieval cities.</cite> It is not a reconstruction, not a theme park — it is a real town that survived the Middle Ages almost intact through pure economic accident.

**How it survived intact:** <cite index="117-1">Rothenburg lost the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) and was forced to pay enormous contributions. The Black Death of 1634 further decimated the population. Complete poverty followed — and because of poverty, there were no funds to replace medieval buildings with newer constructions.</cite> Historical disaster accidentally preserved the town perfectly.

**The medieval wall** — 2.5 km of completely intact walls, with towers, walkways, and views over the town and the Tauber valley. **Free to walk.**

**Town Hall Tower (Rathausturm)** — aerial view over the town. ~€3 entry.

**Altar of the Holy Blood (Heilig-Blut-Altar)** in St. Jakob's Church — <cite index="121-1">carved by Tilman Riemenschneider between 1499–1505, considered one of the masterpieces of German medieval wood sculpture.</cite> A reliquary reportedly containing drops of Christ's blood. Church entry: ~€2.50.

**Night Watchman's Tour** — <cite index="117-1">a one-hour evening walk through Rothenburg led by a costumed guide with a lantern and halberd.</cite> One of Germany's most authentic and popular experiences. **€9 adults, daily at 8:00 PM** (in English and German).

**Insider tip:** Rothenburg is severely overcrowded between 10:00–16:00 in summer. Early morning (8:00–9:30 AM) or after 6:00 PM, when coach tours leave, the town transforms — the same medieval alleys but nearly empty.

## Dinkelsbühl — Rothenburg Without the Crowds

<cite index="121-1">Dinkelsbühl was saved from destruction in the Thirty Years' War and ignored by WWII bombers — it is arguably the Romantic Road's quaintest and most authentically medieval destination.</cite>

Smaller than Rothenburg, with far fewer tourists and the same type of medieval architecture — intact walls, four entry gates, and half-timbered houses from the 15th–16th centuries.

**St. George's Minster** — <cite index="121-1">one of the most important churches in southern Germany, a masterpiece of 15th-century Gothic style.</cite> Free entry.

**Tip:** If Rothenburg is overwhelmed on your visit day, Dinkelsbühl offers 80% of the experience at 20% of the crowd.

## Nördlingen — the Medieval Town Inside a Meteorite Crater

<cite index="118-1">Nördlingen is the Romantic Road's most spectacular geological case: its historic center sits inside a crater formed 14 million years ago by a meteorite impact.</cite> The Nördlinger Ries crater measures 24 km in diameter — one of the best-preserved impact craters in the world.

**Medieval wall (4 km, completely intact)** — can be walked in full in 45–60 minutes. **Free access.**

**Daniel Tower** — the bell tower of St. George's Church, 90 meters, 350 steps, with a panoramic view clearly showing the circular shape of the town inside the crater. ~€3 entry.

**Geological tip:** The stones from which Nördlingen's medieval wall is built contain suevite — a rock formed exclusively through meteorite impacts, containing microscopic diamonds in its composition. The town is literally built from stardust.

## Harburg Castle — the Fortress That Was Never Conquered

<cite index="119-1">Harburg Castle dominates the village of the same name and is definitely worth a visit.</cite> <cite index="99-1">It is considered one of the oldest and best-preserved fortresses in southern Germany — and unlike Neuschwanstein, has never suffered war damage throughout its entire history.</cite>

Built in the 11th century on a hill above the Wörnitz river, Harburg remained in the ownership of the counts of Oettingen-Wallerstein for 700 years — a rare administrative continuity in Europe.

## Augsburg — the Oldest City on the Romantic Road

Augsburg is the route's oldest city — founded by the Romans in 15 BC (Augsburg derives from Augusta Vindelicorum). It has exceptional historical density: the world's first newspaper appeared here (1605), the Fugger banking family practically controlled European finance from Augsburg in the 15th–16th centuries, and Martin Luther was heard here before his excommunication (1518).

**Fuggerei** — the world's first social housing complex, built in 1516 by Jakob Fugger the Rich for the town's poor workers. **Still functional today** — 67 families live in the 140 apartments at a symbolic annual rent of €0.88 (the 1521 value, unchanged for 500 years). The condition: residents must pray for the Fugger family's soul three times daily.

**Fuggerei prices:** ~€6.50 adults (museum + complex).

**Augsburg Cathedral** has the world's oldest figurative stained glass windows — five 11th-century windows depicting Biblical prophets, perfectly preserved. Free entry.

## Neuschwanstein Castle — the Fairy Tale on the Alpine Rock

<cite index="108-1">Neuschwanstein was built for Bavarian King Ludwig II (1845–1886); begun in 1868, never fully completed. The king saw his castle as a monument to the medieval culture he deeply admired and wanted to recreate.</cite>

**The construction story:** Ludwig didn't want a functional castle. He wanted a set for Wagner's operas — <cite index="112-1">the castle was inspired by Wagner's works, German fairy tales, and influenced the design of the Walt Disney logo.</cite> The interiors are a layering of Wagnerian themes: the Throne Room inspired by Byzantine basilicas with a star-studded blue ceiling, the Singers' Hall (Sängersaal) — never used — built for opera performances that never happened, an artificial Cave with electric lighting, and corridors frescoed with scenes from Lohengrin and Parsifal.

<cite index="112-1">Although Neuschwanstein looks like an old castle from the outside, on the inside the king enjoyed central heating, running hot water, telephones, a lift to transport food upstairs, and a system of electric bells to call servants.</cite> It was the most technically advanced "medieval castle" of the 19th century.

Ludwig lived in Neuschwanstein fewer than 200 days. He died 13 days after being arrested and declared insane.

## Hohenschwangau Castle — Where Ludwig II Was Formed

Just 15 minutes' walk from Neuschwanstein, **Hohenschwangau** is the castle where <cite index="108-1">Ludwig II grew up</cite> — built originally in the Middle Ages, partially demolished, and rebuilt by Ludwig's father, King Maximilian II of Bavaria, in 1833.

If Neuschwanstein is Ludwig's fairy tale, Hohenschwangau is the reality he wanted to escape from. And it is more interesting for anyone who wants to understand the psychology of the man behind the fairy-tale castle. The interior decorations — scenes from Lohengrin, chivalric legends, the Holy Grail — shaped the imagination of the child who would become Ludwig II.

**Prices:** <cite index="105-1">€21 adults + €2.50 online booking fee.</cite>

**Important:** <cite index="107-1">Hohenschwangau CANNOT be visited with the Bavarian Palace Annual Pass — it is administered separately.</cite>

**Museum of the Bavarian Kings** — at the foot of the two castles, on the shores of Alpsee. Modern, with audiovisual stations, 10 languages available. **~€12 adults.** Excellent for context before or after the castle visits.

## Linderhof Palace — Ludwig's Pocket Versailles

40 km northeast of Neuschwanstein, hidden in an Alpine valley, <cite index="126-1">Linderhof is Ludwig II's smallest palace but the richest in interior detail — modeled after 18th-century French palaces in Rococo style.</cite>

<cite index="128-1">Its interior is the densest French eighteenth-century quotation Ludwig produced. The Hall of Mirrors is sized for a single occupant rather than a court. The king's bed in the Royal Bedroom faces an audience chamber barred to actual visitors.</cite>

**Venus Grotto** — an artificial cave with an underground lake, lit with colored electric lights, with a shell-shaped boat in which Ludwig would sit and listen to music. Built in 1876, it was one of the few electric installations in Europe at the time.

**The Gardens** — formal, with cascades, fountains, and pergolas, with views of the mountains. The view from the upper garden terrace toward the palace with the Alps in the background is one of Bavaria's defining photographs.

## Herrenchiemsee Palace — The Bavarian Versailles on an Island

<cite index="126-1">Herrenchiemsee, on an island in Lake Chiemsee, was intended to be a replica of the grand Palace of Versailles.</cite> It is Ludwig II's largest project — and his most unfinished. The king died just 9 days after visiting the palace for the first and only time.

**What was completed:** The Hall of Mirrors (slightly longer than the one at Versailles), the State Bedroom (with the most elaborate royal bed in the world), and the formal gardens with 35 fountains.

**What was never built:** Two-thirds of the palace remains in rough structure. You can still see today the unfinished corridors with bare walls — the contrast with the completed rooms is striking.

## Füssen — the Southern End of the Romantic Road

Füssen is the last town on the Romantic Road — at the Austrian border, at the foot of the Alps. <cite index="102-1">The town has a charming old town with colourful buildings and a Benedictine monastery with a spectacular basilica.</cite>

**High Castle (Hohes Schloss)** — former summer residence of the Bishops of Augsburg, with an art gallery and the best aerial view of the town. ~€6 entry.

**Neuschwanstein and Hohenschwangau** — 4 km from Füssen, by bus 73/78 (~€2.80).

## Essential practical tips

### Booking Neuschwanstein — the correct system

<cite index="106-1">Book your ticket online with an entry time between 9:15–10:30 AM.</cite> The exact process:
1. Go to **hohenschwangau.de** → Tickets → Online Shop
2. Select date and time (5-minute interval — you cannot enter before or after)
3. Pay by card: €21 ticket + €2.50 booking fee
4. Receive QR code by email
5. At entrance scan QR code — complete skip-the-line

**Without reservation:** go to the Ticket Center Hohenschwangau very early (7:30–8:00 AM) for same-day tickets. Availability varies — not guaranteed in summer.

### Photography in the castles

**Interior photography banned** at Neuschwanstein — the tour guide warns you at entry. Respect the rule: the interiors are spectacular but the 30-minute tour moves quickly and the guide doesn't stop for photographs.

**Permitted** outdoors, at Marienbrücke, and in Linderhof's gardens.

### Best season to visit

| Period | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| December–February | Snow on Neuschwanstein (most beautiful winter), low prices | Cold, reduced hours |
| March–May | Few tourists, good prices | Possibly rainy |
| June–August | Everything open, warm | Most crowded, book months ahead |
| September–October | **Ideal** — autumn colors in the mountains, smaller crowds | Linderhof gardens reduce |
| November | Complete peace, low prices | Some pavilions closed |

**Neuschwanstein in winter** — the snow-covered castle is an image you know before you've ever seen it: white turrets against a backdrop of snowy Alps. A clear winter day in December or January is more photogenic than any crowded summer.

## Insider Tips

**1. Neuschwanstein in winter is better than summer.** Snow on the turrets, no crowds, and crystal-clear blue sky make December–January the most photogenic season. Verify the castle is open (closed December 24–25 and December 31–January 1).

**2. The interior tour at Neuschwanstein lasts exactly 30 minutes.** No more, no less. The guide moves fast, doesn't stop for photographs (banned anyway). If you want to understand what you're seeing, read about the castle's rooms in advance.

**3. Marienbrücke can be closed.** The bridge closes in dangerous weather conditions or for repairs. Check in advance. If closed, there is an alternative viewpoint on the forest path to the left (partial view but free).

**4. Rothenburg in the evening is different.** The Night Watchman Tour at 8:00 PM (in English) costs €9 and is the only absolutely recommended guided tour — the guide has been doing this tour for 25 years. The medieval atmosphere by lantern light is incomparable.

**5. Dinkelsbühl is Rothenburg without the surcharge.** If Rothenburg exhausts you with crowds and prices, Dinkelsbühl is 50 km south, architecturally identical, with 90% fewer tourists.

**6. Fuggerei in Augsburg is more impressive than it sounds.** The 1516 social housing complex, still functioning today with its €0.88 annual rent, is a concept more fascinating than any castle. Allow 45 minutes.

**7. Linderhof is better than Neuschwanstein for photography.** Photography is permitted in Linderhof's gardens and exterior. The Venus Grotto and functioning fountains (in summer) are spectacular.
