You land at the departure board. Your flight says DELAYED or CANCELLED. The first instinct is to queue at the airline desk and accept whatever they offer. Before you do anything, read this: you may be owed up to €600 per person — and most passengers never claim it.

EU Regulation 261/2004 has been law since February 2005, yet airlines routinely ignore it, and passengers rarely know their rights. This guide covers everything: what you're owed, when you qualify, what airlines can legitimately avoid paying, and the two fastest ways to get your money in 2026.

## What Is EU Regulation 261/2004?

EU Regulation 261/2004 (often called "EU261") is European law that gives air passengers fixed compensation rights when:

- Their flight is **delayed by 3+ hours** at the final destination
- Their flight is **cancelled with less than 14 days' notice**
- They are **denied boarding** due to overbooking

The compensation is automatic — it doesn't depend on the airline's goodwill, your ticket type, or whether you complained. It's a legal obligation.

**Updated for 2026:** The European Parliament voted in January 2026 on proposed amendments to EU261, including clearer definitions of "extraordinary circumstances" and updated distance thresholds. Until the final adoption (expected late 2026 or 2027), the current rules remain fully in force.

## Where Does EU261 Apply?

The regulation covers:

- **Any flight departing from an EU airport**, regardless of which airline operates it (including non-EU carriers like Emirates, Turkish Airlines, American Airlines)
- **Flights arriving at an EU airport**, if operated by an EU-based airline (Wizz Air, Ryanair, Lufthansa, Tarom, Air France, etc.)

**UK:** After Brexit, the UK adopted its own equivalent — **UK Regulation 261** — with almost identical rules. Claims for UK departures or UK-operated flights arriving in the UK follow the same logic and compensation amounts (paid in GBP equivalent).

**Norway, Iceland, Switzerland:** All three have adopted EU261 as part of their EEA/bilateral agreements.

**What's NOT covered:** A Delta Airlines flight from New York to Bucharest delayed at JFK — non-EU airline, non-EU departure. But Lufthansa Frankfurt–New York delayed at FRA — covered (EU carrier departing EU airport).

## Compensation Amounts — The Full Table

| Flight Distance | Minimum Delay | Compensation | Example Routes from Bucharest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 1,500 km | 3 hours | **€250** | Vienna, Budapest, Sofia, Athens, Istanbul |
| 1,500–3,500 km | 3 hours | **€400** | London, Paris, Barcelona, Rome, Tel Aviv |
| Over 3,500 km | 4 hours | **€600** | New York, Dubai, Bangkok, Tokyo |

**Critical:** The amounts are **per passenger**. A family of four on a delayed Bucharest–London flight can claim €1,600 total (4 × €400).

The compensation is completely independent of your ticket price. A €25 Ryanair promo fare on a 2,000 km route delayed 4 hours = €400. Same as a €400 flexible business fare.

## When Airlines DON'T Have to Pay — Extraordinary Circumstances

The one legitimate escape clause for airlines is "extraordinary circumstances" — events genuinely outside their control that couldn't have been avoided even if all reasonable measures had been taken.

**Qualifies as extraordinary circumstances:**
- Severe weather conditions (major storms, fog, volcanic ash like Eyjafjallajökull)
- Air Traffic Control (ATC) strikes — not airline staff strikes
- Confirmed security threats (bomb threats, terrorism)
- Airport infrastructure failures outside airline control
- Medical emergencies on board causing diversion

**Does NOT qualify (airlines frequently try this — courts disagree):**
- Technical aircraft faults and mechanical failures
- Airline staff strikes (pilots, cabin crew, ground handling)
- Crew scheduling failures and rostering errors
- Overbooking
- Late arrival of the aircraft from a previous flight ("knock-on delays")

The European Court of Justice has ruled repeatedly that technical faults are part of the normal operation of an airline and cannot constitute extraordinary circumstances. When airlines claim this, they often lose — but you have to challenge them.

**Practical tip:** When an airline says "technical issue — no compensation," challenge it. Statistics from AirHelp show that 70–80% of initially rejected claims based on "technical faults" are later successful when properly challenged.

## Your Care Rights — Even Below the 3-Hour Threshold

Even if your delay is under 3 hours, you have legally mandated care rights the moment your flight is delayed:

**After 2 hours of delay (short flights under 1,500 km):**
- Meals and refreshments proportionate to the waiting time
- 2 free telephone calls, emails, or faxes

**After 3 hours of delay (flights 1,500–3,500 km):**
- Same meals and refreshments

**After 4 hours of delay (long-haul flights):**
- Same meals and refreshments

**If the delay goes overnight:**
- Hotel accommodation
- Transport between the airport and hotel (both ways)

**After 5 hours of delay — if you choose not to travel:**
- Full refund of your ticket price (including return journey if you're already at the destination and the delay makes the outbound pointless)

If the airline fails to provide these and you purchase food/accommodation yourself, **keep all receipts** — you can claim these costs back in addition to the financial compensation.

## What to Do at the Airport — Step by Step

If your flight is delayed or cancelled, the order of your actions matters:

**Step 1: Document immediately.** Photograph the departures board showing your flight status, the time, and any reason stated. Note the exact scheduled departure and the announced delay/cancellation time.

**Step 2: Get written confirmation from the airline.** Ask at the desk for written confirmation of the reason for delay or cancellation. They're legally required to provide it. If they refuse, the photograph of the board is your evidence.

**Step 3: Request your care rights.** Ask for meal vouchers. If the airline doesn't offer them and you're waiting 2+ hours, buy food and keep the receipts.

**Step 4: Accept a replacement flight — but DON'T waive your compensation.** If the airline offers an alternative flight, you can accept it. Accepting the alternative does NOT cancel your right to €250–€600 compensation — these are legally separate entitlements.

**Step 5: Do NOT sign compensation waivers.** Airlines sometimes offer vouchers "in exchange for" compensation rights. Read everything carefully. If a document says you're accepting a voucher "in full and final settlement," you may be signing away your legal right to cash.

**Step 6: File your claim within a reasonable time.** Technically you have 3 years (in Romania), but the sooner the better. Details are fresher, evidence is intact.

## How to Claim — Three Options

### Option 1: Directly to the Airline (Free, Takes Effort)

Every EU airline is required to have a complaint form. Send a written claim via email or their online form, including:
- Flight number
- Scheduled date and time
- Actual arrival time at final destination
- Reason communicated by the airline
- Explicit request for compensation under EU Regulation 261/2004

Airlines must respond within 6 weeks. If they don't, or they refuse without a valid extraordinary circumstances defence, you escalate.

**Pros:** You keep 100% of the compensation.

**Cons:** Airlines routinely ignore, delay, or issue automatic refusals on the first claim. The process can take 3–12 months and may require additional correspondence, complaints to national aviation authorities (ANPC in Romania, CAA in the UK), and potentially small claims court.

## Special Cases That Catch Passengers Off Guard

### Missed Connections on a Single Booking

If you had Bucharest → Frankfurt → Los Angeles on one ticket, and your first leg delayed caused you to miss the Frankfurt connection, compensation is calculated on the **total journey distance** (Bucharest–Los Angeles = ~10,000 km = €600 per passenger).

The condition: it must be a single booking/PNR. If you bought two separate tickets and missed your connection, EU261 doesn't apply between the two separate contracts.

### Return Flights

Your outbound and return flights are treated as **separate journeys**, each with independent compensation rights. A cancelled return flight from London to Bucharest is a separate claim from your outbound journey.

### Flights Operated by a Different Airline Than Who You Booked With

If you booked with Lufthansa but the flight was operated by Brussels Airlines (code-share), the **operating carrier** is the one liable under EU261 — even though you have a Lufthansa booking reference. The marketing carrier (who sold you the ticket) shares secondary liability.

### Overnight Delay Turning Into Next-Day Flight

If your flight is delayed until the next day, you're entitled to hotel accommodation AND the full financial compensation (if total delay at destination is 3+ hours). Both rights apply simultaneously.
