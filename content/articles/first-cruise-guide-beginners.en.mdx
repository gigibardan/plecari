A cruise is unlike any other holiday. It is not a hotel and not a tour — it is a hybrid with its own logic, vocabulary, and unwritten rules. If this is your first one, you are about to discover something that converts a remarkable proportion of people into repeat cruisers. You are also about to encounter a category of travel with more opportunities for unexpected costs than almost anything else. This guide covers both sides.

## What a cruise actually is (and is not)

The concept is simple: you sleep on the ship, the ship moves between destinations while you sleep, and you wake up somewhere new each morning. One suitcase, no check-outs, six ports in seven nights.

What makes cruising unusual is that the ship is simultaneously your hotel, your transport between destinations, and — if you choose — your primary source of entertainment, dining, and activity. You do not have to do anything. You can spend the entire voyage on deck, eating at the buffet, watching shows in the theatre, and considering whether to get in the pool. Or you can be off the ship at every port from 8 AM to 5 PM, treating the ship purely as a floating hotel.

Most first-time cruisers land somewhere between these extremes and discover the rhythm suits them well.

## What is included — and what is not

This is the source of the biggest surprises on a first cruise. The cabin price covers more than a hotel room, but less than most people assume.

**Included in the cabin price:**
- Your cabin for the duration of the cruise
- Main meals: breakfast, lunch and dinner at the buffet (open most of the day) and the main dining room (set dinner times or freestyle, depending on the line)
- Onboard entertainment: theatre shows, musical performances, live music, trivia nights, pool activities, game shows
- Use of all common areas: pools, deck chairs, gym, library, walking track
- Access to all ports on the itinerary

**NOT included — and these add up:**
- **Alcoholic beverages:** beer €4–7, cocktails €8–14, wine €6–10/glass onboard. A moderate drinker easily spends €30–50/day.
- **Drinks package:** most cruise lines sell a flat-rate all-inclusive drinks package (€40–65/person/day). Worth it if you drink 3–4 alcoholic drinks plus specialty coffees per day. Not worth it for light drinkers.
- **Specialty restaurants:** beyond the included main dining room and buffet, ships have Italian, Asian, steakhouse, and sushi restaurants that charge €15–45/person.
- **Mandatory service charge (gratuity):** €10–15/person/day, added automatically to your onboard account. On a 7-night cruise for two people: €140–210.
- **Shore excursions:** organized tours in port cost €40–150/person/excursion.
- **Wi-Fi:** €20–35/day or €80–150 for the voyage. Slow by land standards.
- **Spa and treatments:** massages €80–130, hair, nails — all charged separately.
- **Photographs:** professional photographers are everywhere on the ship. Their packages cost €30–150.
- **Flights to the embarkation port and hotel the night before.**

**The real cost calculation:** a 7-night cruise advertised at €400/person in an interior cabin typically costs €800–1,200/person all-in once you add the gratuity, a few drinks, two or three excursions, and travel to/from the port.

## How embarkation works — the first day

Embarkation is the process of boarding the ship. It is more elaborate than checking into a hotel, and arriving without understanding the process causes unnecessary stress.

**The timeline:**
- Embarkation window opens 3–4 hours before departure (check your documents for the specific time)
- You must be fully boarded at least 90 minutes before departure (ships have a hard cutoff)
- The ship departs at the stated time. Exceptions: almost never.

**What happens when you arrive at the port:**

**Step 1 — Drop luggage:** porters at the pier take your large bags and deliver them to your cabin within 1–3 hours. Carry anything you need immediately (medication, valuables, a change of clothes) in your hand luggage.

**Step 2 — Check-in:** join the check-in line with your passport and booking confirmation. Staff scan documents, take your photo, and issue your sea pass card.

**Step 3 — The sea pass card:** this single card does everything for the rest of the cruise — it opens your cabin door, identifies you to crew, and functions as a charge account for everything you buy onboard. Nothing on the ship costs cash; everything goes on the sea pass and is settled against your credit card at the end of the voyage.

**Step 4 — Board and explore:** first-timers often spend the first hour simply walking the ship. The scale is disorienting (large ships span 15+ decks and 300+ metres in length) and finding your way around takes a day.

**Step 5 — Muster drill (lifeboat safety briefing):** mandatory before the ship departs, no exceptions. Check your documents for the time and location. It takes 20–30 minutes.

**The golden rule of embarkation day:** do not fly to the port city on embarkation day. Book a flight for the day before and stay overnight near the port. If your flight is delayed — even by 3 hours — you miss the ship. The ship will not hold for you, regardless of the circumstances. One hotel night near the port (€60–120) is the cheapest insurance available.

## Choosing your cabin — the most important booking decision

Your cabin category determines the price more than almost any other factor. Understanding the differences helps you get the right one for your situation.

### Interior cabin

No window. Complete darkness. Usually 14–18 square metres. The cheapest category by far — 40–60% less than a balcony on the same itinerary.

**Who should choose it:** first-time cruisers, budget-conscious travellers, anyone who sleeps deeply, solo travellers, groups of friends splitting the cost. The complete darkness is actually a sleeping advantage — many people report sleeping better in an interior cabin than they do at home.

The case for the interior cabin is simple: you will spend perhaps 8 hours/day in the cabin, mostly asleep. The other 16 hours, you are in the dining room, on deck, in ports, at shows. The cabin is a place to sleep, not to live.

### Oceanview cabin

A window (sealed, does not open). Natural light, visual connection to the sea. 30–40% more expensive than interior. The middle option that satisfies the desire for light without the balcony premium.

### Balcony cabin

A sliding glass door onto a private outdoor space (typically 6–10 square metres). You can eat breakfast outside, watch the ship arrive in port, and sit with a drink watching the sun go down. 50–80% more expensive than interior.

**Who should choose it:** second or third-time cruisers who know they want the outdoor space, couples on a special occasion, anyone cruising a genuinely scenic route (Norwegian fjords, Alaska, Greek islands from a smaller ship) where the visual experience between ports matters.

### Suite

A separate living area, a significantly larger balcony, priority embarkation, dedicated concierge service, and in many cases access to a private restaurant and sun deck. 150–300% more expensive than interior. For people who want a cruise experience that is unambiguously luxurious.

## Shore excursions: organised vs independent

This is the category with the highest price variation and the most opportunity to either waste money or have exceptional experiences.

**Ship-organised excursions:**
- Priced at €45–150/person for a half-day
- The ship guarantees departure if the excursion runs late — if you are on a ship excursion and the bus breaks down, the ship waits
- Groups of 30–50 people, fixed route, limited time at each stop
- Quality varies significantly — some are excellent, many are generic

**Independent exploration:**
- Walk off the ship and go where you want
- Significantly cheaper for most ports
- Full freedom — you stop where you want, stay as long as you like
- The risk: the ship will not wait for you if you are late. Know the departure time and allow a 45-minute buffer.

**GetYourGuide and local tour operators:**
The middle ground — professional guided tours with smaller groups (8–15 people), at 40–60% of the ship's price for comparable content. Book in advance, not at the dock.

**The practical decision per port:**

| Port | Recommendation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Rome (Civitavecchia) | Independent or GetYourGuide | 1.5h train to Rome, easy independently |
| Pompeii (Naples) | Guided tour | Context transforms the site |
| Athens (Piraeus) | Independent | Metro to Acropolis, easy and cheap |
| Santorini | Independent | Walk to Oia, take the cable car, explore freely |
| Dubrovnik | Independent | Walk the walls yourself |
| Ephesus (Kusadasi) | Ship excursion or guided | Complex site, guide adds enormous value |
| Barcelona | Independent or GetYourGuide | Bus/metro is excellent |

## What happens on a typical day — port day vs sea day

**A port day:**
- 06:30–07:30: wake up, buffet breakfast (the ship docks while most are still sleeping)
- 07:30–08:30: disembark (on foot, or by tender boat if the ship is anchored offshore)
- 08:30–16:00: explore the port city or organised excursion
- 16:00–17:00: return to the ship (check exact departure time — it varies per port)
- Evening: dinner on the ship, evening show, bar

**A sea day:**
- Morning: buffet breakfast at leisure, then pool, deck chairs, gym, or spa
- Lunch at the buffet or main dining room
- Afternoon: scheduled activities (trivia, dance lessons, cooking demonstrations, lectures), or quiet time on deck
- Evening: main dining room dinner, then evening show or casino

Most 7-night Mediterranean itineraries have 4–5 port days and 2–3 sea days. Sea days are often underrated by first-timers and come to be genuinely enjoyed by most people — the pace drops completely and the ship itself becomes the destination.

## Dining on the ship

Cruise ships feed thousands of people multiple times a day. The logistics are impressive; the quality varies considerably between lines and between venues.

**The main dining room:** the formal (but not stuffy) sit-down restaurant included in the price. Three-course dinners with a changing menu each night. Most lines have two sittings (early, around 6 PM, and late, around 8:30 PM) — select your preference at booking. Norwegian Cruise Line's "Freestyle Dining" system allows dining any time, anywhere, without reservations.

**The buffet:** open for breakfast, lunch, and most of the afternoon. The most convenient option and genuinely good for breakfast across all lines. Quality at dinner is lower.

**Specialty restaurants:** extra charge, reservation required, significantly better quality. Italian, Asian, steakhouse, seafood — depending on the ship. Worth trying once, particularly on longer voyages.

**Room service:** available on most ships, often free during meal service hours and charged a small fee overnight. Useful for sea-day mornings when you don't want to get dressed.

## Communications: the Wi-Fi trap and how to avoid it

Ship Wi-Fi is expensive (€20–35/day, or €80–150 for the voyage) and slow (satellite connection shared among thousands of passengers). For most travellers, there is a far better solution.

**The eSIM approach:** buy a data eSIM before departure. It works in every port (where you actually need internet — for maps, translations, restaurant searches), costs €5–15 for the entire cruise period across multiple countries, and gives you proper 4G speeds rather than slow satellite. In the ports where you need it most, you have it. In open sea, you don't — but you don't really need it there either.

**WhatsApp on ship Wi-Fi:** if you do purchase Wi-Fi, most modern cruise ships offer a "social media" or "messaging" package at a lower price than full internet — covers WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, but not browsing or streaming. Useful for staying in touch with family.

## The 10 things nobody tells you before your first cruise

**1. The onboard account is always open.** Everything goes on the sea pass — drinks, spa, excursions, specialty dining, Wi-Fi. The total is charged to your credit card at the end. Check your account daily via the ship's app or a kiosk. It is easy to spend significantly more than you planned without noticing until the end.

**2. The single supplement is brutal.** Cabins are priced per person based on double occupancy. If you travel alone, you typically pay for two people — the "single supplement" adds 50–100% to the cost. Some lines (Norwegian, Virgin Voyages) have specific solo cabins or waiver programmes.

**3. Smoking rules are strict.** No smoking in cabins, on balconies, or in most public areas. Designated smoking zones exist (typically on one side of an open deck). Violating this policy in a cabin results in significant fines.

**4. Bring a power strip.** Most cabins have 1–2 US-style sockets and sometimes one European socket. With phones, tablets, and cameras all needing charging, a compact multi-socket extension is invaluable. Cruise line rules prohibit those with surge protectors — bring a simple strip only.

**5. The dress code is more relaxed than you think.** During the day: casual, always. Evenings: most lines have 1–2 "smart casual" or "formal" nights. Smart casual means no shorts or flip flops in the main dining room — trousers and a shirt for men, a dress or smart top for women. You won't be turned away in casual clothes, but the ambiance of the dining room on a formal night is one of the more pleasant social rituals of cruising.

**6. Debarkation is slow.** The last morning — when all passengers leave the ship — is not quick. You are assigned a debarkation time based on your cabin and flight schedule. The process can take 1–2 hours from when you wake up to when you are actually off the ship and through the terminal. Do not book a flight before 11 AM on debarkation day.

**7. The itinerary can change.** Ships skip ports due to weather, mechanical issues, or port availability. It is written into the contract. A port substituted for another, or a sea day replacing a port day — you have no recourse. This happens rarely on Mediterranean itineraries in summer but is worth knowing.

**8. The gym is free and usually well-equipped.** Most large ships have extensive gym facilities included. The gym on a large MSC or Norwegian ship can rival a good city gym. Spa treatments adjacent to the gym are charged separately.

**9. Early dinner seating fills up with families and retirees.** If you prefer a livelier atmosphere at dinner, choose the late seating (8:30 PM) or freestyle dining if available. The later crowd tends to be younger couples and those who have been out at the ports until the afternoon.

**10. The last port is often the best.** By the final port day, you have the logistics figured out, you know how much time you actually have, and you are confident about returning on time. Most first-time cruisers find the last port day the most enjoyable.

## Travel insurance for a cruise — more important than for any other holiday

Cruises have the strictest cancellation policies in travel (up to 100% forfeiture within 14 days of sailing) and the highest potential costs in a medical emergency (helicopter evacuation from a ship can cost €15,000–50,000).

A travel insurance policy for a cruise should specifically cover:
- **Trip cancellation** at 100% of the cruise fare for covered reasons
- **Medical evacuation at sea** — explicitly, not just "emergency transport"
- **Missed port departure** — covers transport costs if you miss the ship in a port
- **Cabin confinement** — compensation if you are medically confined to your cabin
- **Cruise interruption** — if you need to disembark mid-cruise for a medical reason

Standard travel insurance policies often have gaps for cruise-specific scenarios. Check explicitly before purchasing.

**What to book:**
- 7-night Mediterranean itinerary for a first cruise (ideal length — long enough to settle in, short enough not to feel trapped if you discover you don't love it)
- Interior cabin (save 40–60% over balcony; invest the difference in excursions)
- Embarkation from Athens or Rome (short flights from most of Europe, good first-port experiences)
- 6–8 months ahead for the best prices; or last-minute if your dates are completely flexible

**What to do immediately after booking:**
1. Buy travel insurance (the same day — coverage begins from the policy issue date)
2. Book your flight for the day before embarkation
3. Book a hotel near the embarkation port for the night before
4. Complete online check-in through the cruise line's app (opens 30–60 days before sailing)
5. Research each port independently — 30 minutes per port before departure transforms the experience
