Venice appears from the water like a mirage — a city floating on the surface of a lagoon, its domes and campanili rising above the horizon with no visible foundation. When you step off the vaporetto from the airport and walk into the calli (narrow streets) for the first time, the complete absence of cars creates a silence that feels impossible for a city of this size. The only sounds are footsteps, the slap of water against stone, and the distant echo of bells from one of the 100+ churches.

Venice is also the most photographed, most visited, and most imitated city in the world — a city of 25 million annual visitors descending on a residential population of roughly 50,000. Understanding the tension between these two realities is the key to visiting it well.

This guide covers the city that exists between (and often beneath) the tourist layer: the six sestieri and what each one actually contains, the 2026 access fee explained honestly, getting around by vaporetto, the major sites with the detail they deserve, the cicchetti culture that most visitors miss entirely, and the islands of the lagoon.

## The 2026 Access Fee — The Full, Honest Explanation

Venice introduced the Contributo di Accesso (Access Contribution) as a day-visitor management measure. The 2026 reality is more nuanced than most headlines suggest:

**Who pays:**
- Day visitors (arriving and leaving the same day) on designated peak dates: **€5 if booked in advance** at veneziaunica.it, **€10 if not booked**

**Who does NOT pay:**
- Overnight guests in Venice hotels, B&Bs, or apartments — you are explicitly exempt. Your accommodation already charges a tourist tax (€1–5 per person per night, paid separately at check-in).
- Children under 14
- Residents, workers, and students in Venice
- Visitors for specific non-tourism purposes (medical appointments, religious events)

**When does it apply:**
- Primarily on **weekends and Italian public holidays between April and July** — the highest-demand day-visitor dates. Not every day of the year. Check veneziaunica.it for the specific calendar of dates.

**Enforcement:**
- Random checks at entry points: Santa Lucia train station, Piazzale Roma, some vaporetto stops
- Inspectors check QR codes generated when you register and pay
- Fines for non-compliance: **€50–300**

**The practical implication:** If you're staying in Venice overnight — which this guide recommends — you don't deal with this. If you're doing a day trip from the mainland on a summer weekend, book the €5 fee in advance.

## Understanding Venice — The City Before You Arrive

Venice is built across 118 small islands in a lagoon on the Adriatic coast of northeastern Italy. The islands are connected by approximately 400 bridges and threaded by 150+ canals. There are no cars, no motorcycles, no bicycles (technically permitted but barely practical). Everything moves on foot or on water.

**The six sestieri (districts):**
Venice is divided into six historic districts. Understanding which is which transforms the experience:

- **San Marco:** The tourist core — St Mark's, the Doge's Palace, the Rialto, the Grand Canal frontage. Extraordinary and unavoidable. Also the most crowded, most expensive, and least residential part of the city.
- **Dorsoduro:** Across the Grand Canal from San Marco. Art galleries (Gallerie dell'Accademia, Guggenheim), the Ca' Rezzonico, the Zattere waterfront, students, and some of the city's best sunset light. The most liveable neighbourhood for visitors.
- **San Polo:** The oldest district, home to the Rialto market (operating since 1097) and the Frari church. Between San Marco and the train station, compact and walkable.
- **Santa Croce:** Gateway from the train station and car parks, home to the Ca' Pesaro museum, less visited by tourists.
- **Cannaregio:** The most residential and most authentically Venetian sestiere. The world's first Jewish Ghetto (established 1516), the Fondamenta della Misericordia (the best canal-side street for cicchetti and aperitivo), and the northern lagoon waterfront.
- **Castello:** The largest sestiere, stretching east past San Marco to the Arsenal (Venice's historic shipyard, the largest industrial complex of the medieval world) and the public gardens (Giardini, home to the Venice Biennale). The eastern areas are entirely residential — locals playing football, parks, hidden gardens. Venice without filters.

**Navigation note:** Street addresses in Venice use the sestiere name plus a number (e.g., "Cannaregio 2345") — this is a sequential numbering system within each sestiere, not a street name. Google Maps and Citymapper handle Venetian navigation well using points of interest. Paper maps are useful backup.

**Shoes:** Venice is walked. Over a typical day you'll cross 50–100 bridges (each has steps) and cover 8–15 km on irregular stone surfaces. Bring proper walking shoes. Not sandals, not fashion trainers — shoes with support and grip.

## The Major Sites

### St Mark's Basilica (Basilica di San Marco)

The most extraordinary church exterior in Europe — five domes and an entirely gilded mosaic facade visible from the piazza. Built in the 11th century as the Doge's private chapel (not originally a cathedral — it became Venice's cathedral only in 1807), it reflects Venice's unique position at the intersection of Byzantine East and Latin West.

**The interior:** The entire upper surface — walls, arches, domes — is covered with Byzantine gold mosaic, 8,000 m² of it, representing scenes from the Old and New Testaments in a continuous visual narrative. When sunlight enters the high windows at certain times of day, the gold appears to radiate — the effect described by Henry James as "the glow of a golden cavern."

**The Pala d'Oro:** Behind the main altar, an altarpiece assembled over several centuries from Byzantine gold panels, enamels, and gems — 250 enamelled plaques, 1,927 stones (pearls, rubies, emeralds, sapphires), and a gold weight of several kilograms. Entry to see the Pala d'Oro requires a separate ticket (included in most skip-the-line tours).

**The Terrazza (Terrace):** Steps from the nave lead to the external terrace from which you look out across St Mark's Square at rooftop level. The original bronze horses (copies stand outside; originals are inside the Museo Marciano) can be seen at close range. The perspective of the square from above changes your understanding of its scale.

**Queue reality:** Without a pre-booked ticket, the standard queue in peak season is 1.5–3 hours. Skip-the-line tickets enter through a separate gate and typically take 10–15 minutes. Book via veneziaunica.it or GetYourGuide. Entry: €12–15 depending on what's included.

**The key timing secret:** Basilica San Marco opens at 09:30 daily (later on Sundays for Mass — tourists typically admitted from 14:00). The first 30 minutes after opening are dramatically less crowded. For the terrace, which opens separately at a different time, check the official site before visiting.

### Doge's Palace (Palazzo Ducale)

The seat of Venetian government for almost 1,000 years — from roughly 810 AD until Napoleon ended the Venetian Republic in 1797. It's one of the most significant Gothic buildings in Europe and the political centre of what was, at its peak, the most powerful commercial empire in the world.

**The building:** The famous pink and white diamond-patterned marble exterior, the courtyard with the Giants' Staircase (up which newly elected Doges were crowned), the breathtaking Hall of the Great Council (the Sala del Maggior Consiglio — 54 metres long, decorated with Tintoretto's Paradise, the largest canvas painting in the world), the Sala del Senato and the Sala del Collegio — all painted by Tintoretto, Veronese, and Tiepolo.

**The Bridge of Sighs (Ponte dei Sospiri):** The enclosed white limestone bridge connecting the palace to the New Prisons (1600). The name — supposedly the sighs of condemned prisoners taking their last view of Venice through the small windows — was coined by Byron in the 19th century and has no contemporary historical record. The real history: the bridge connected the interrogation rooms to the prison, and prisoners' sighs were very real. You cross it as part of the palace visit.

**The Doge's Palace Secret Itinerary (Itinerari Segreti):** This is the tour to book. A 75-minute guided tour through areas closed to general visitors:
- The state inquisitors' offices (the three-member committee that controlled Venice's internal security)
- The torture chamber (the guaranzone or "question" room — where suspects were interrogated, sometimes physically)
- Casanova's cell in the Piombi (the "Leads" — the prison in the roof, named for its lead-covered ceiling, extraordinarily hot in summer and freezing in winter)
- The route of Casanova's escape in 1756 — the only successful escape from the Piombi in its history

Giacomo Casanova was imprisoned in the Piombi in 1755, accused of being a spy, Freemason, and practitioner of magic. He spent 14 months planning an escape that involved chipping a hole through the floor with an iron bar, hiding in a nearby room, and eventually climbing across the palace roof before being let out of a locked room by a passing official who thought he was a civil servant locked in accidentally. His account of the escape, written years later, is one of the great adventure memoirs of the 18th century.

**Entry:** Standard: €30. Secret Itinerary tour: €32 (includes standard palace). Book at palazzoducale.visitmuve.it — the Secret Itinerary books out weeks ahead in season.

### The Grand Canal

The 3.8 km reverse-S shaped main canal through the city, lined with approximately 200 palazzi (palaces) from the 13th–18th centuries. The Grand Canal is essentially Venice's high street, where the wealthy trading families of the Republic displayed their wealth in architectural competition.

**The most accessible viewing is from the vaporetto:** Line 1 travels the entire length of the Grand Canal, stopping at every landing. A single vaporetto ticket (€9.50) gives you a 50-minute journey along the world's most extraordinary waterway. The best times: early morning (06:00–08:00, cargo boats making deliveries), late afternoon (17:00–18:00, golden light on the palazzi facades), and at night (the palaces are lit from the water).

**Key palazzi to identify:**
- Ca' d'Oro (Gothic, 15th century — "the golden house," named for its originally gilded facade)
- Ca' Pesaro (Baroque, 17th century — now the International Gallery of Modern Art and the Oriental Museum)
- Ca' Rezzonico (Baroque, 18th century — now the Museum of 18th-Century Venice)
- Palazzo Grassi (acquired by François Pinault, now a contemporary art museum)
- Punta della Dogana (the customs house at the tip of Dorsoduro, also Pinault's contemporary art space)

**Rialto Bridge:** The most famous bridge in Venice, connecting San Polo and San Marco across the Grand Canal. Completed in 1591 after a competition in which Michelangelo, Palladio, Sansovino, and ultimately Antonio da Ponte submitted designs. The bridge's single arch was considered structurally daring at the time. The shops along the bridge have been there since its construction. Cross it at dawn for the unencumbered view.

### Gallerie dell'Accademia

Venice's principal art museum, tracing Venetian painting from the Byzantine period through the 18th century. In a city full of art in churches and palaces, the Accademia provides the chronological framework to understand it.

**Must-sees:** Bellini's altarpieces (the finest examples of the painter who defined Venetian colour); Carpaccio's Cycle of St Ursula (nine large narrative canvases, remarkably detailed depictions of 15th-century Venice); Giorgione's The Tempest (one of the great unsolved puzzles of Renaissance art — nobody knows what it depicts); Veronese's Feast in the House of Levi (monumental, 5m × 13m, originally painted as The Last Supper until the Inquisition objected to the drunks and dogs Veronese included — he renamed it rather than repaint it); Titian's Pietà (his last work, intended for his own tomb).

**Entry:** €15. Book in advance at gallerieaccademia.it or via GetYourGuide. Closed Mondays.

### Peggy Guggenheim Collection

The American heiress Peggy Guggenheim bought the unfinished Palazzo Venier dei Leoni in 1949 and filled it with her extraordinary collection of 20th-century art — now one of the most important collections of modernism in Europe. The palazzo's low profile earned it the nickname "palazzo nonfinito" (unfinished palace).

Picasso, Braque, Léger, Mondrian, Kandinsky, Dalí, Ernst (whom Guggenheim briefly married), Pollock (whom she discovered and funded), and Marini's equestrian sculpture The Angel of the City on the Grand Canal terrace — a bronze horseman with a frankly erect phallus, which Guggenheim reportedly detached on days when nuns passed by on the water.

**Entry:** €18. On the Dorsoduro waterfront, a 5-minute walk from the Accademia. Closed Tuesdays.

## The Sestieri — Venice Beyond the Postcards

### Dorsoduro — Art and Aperitivo

Dorsoduro ("hard ridge" — the highest and most stable ground in Venice) is the sestiere that serious Venetian visitors base themselves in. The Zattere waterfront — a long sun-drenched fondamenta facing south toward the Giudecca island — is one of the great places to sit in Venice: wide, accessible, and catching afternoon light until sunset. The art cluster (Accademia, Guggenheim, Ca' Rezzonico, Punta della Dogana, Palazzo Grassi across the canal) makes it the most culturally concentrated neighbourhood outside San Marco.

**Campo Santa Margherita:** The heartbeat of Dorsoduro — a large, irregular square lined with cafés and bars, populated by university students from Ca' Foscari. The best spritz prices in Venice (€3–5 vs €8–12 near San Marco). An evening in Campo Santa Margherita with a Aperol Spritz and a plate of cicchetti is the authentic local alternative to overpriced San Marco terraces.

### Cannaregio — The Real Venice

The most authentically Venetian sestiere. As you walk north from the train station, the tourist infrastructure gradually disappears. By the time you reach Fondamenta della Misericordia, you're in a working neighbourhood where Venetians go for evening aperitivo at canal-side bacari.

**The Jewish Ghetto of Venice (Ghetto Veneziano):** Established by the Venetian Republic in 1516, this is the world's first formal Jewish ghetto — the origin of the word "ghetto" itself (from the Venetian geto, "foundry," since the area had been an iron foundry). The Jewish community was confined here at night, with gates locked by Christian guards. Despite the confinement, the community contributed substantially to Venetian finance, scholarship, and trade. The buildings are noticeably taller than the rest of Venice — built upward because land area was fixed by law but the population grew. Five synagogues survive within the ghetto, four of which can be visited on a guided tour. The Museo Ebraico (Jewish Museum) on Campo del Ghetto Nuovo: €12.

**Fondamenta della Misericordia:** The canal-side street that locals consider the best in Venice for aperitivo. Low-key bacari, canal views, no tourist premium pricing, and a genuine neighbourhood atmosphere — the same local bar that's been serving Venetians since the 1960s next to a craft aperitivo bar opened last year.

**Madonna dell'Orto:** Tintoretto's parish church — the great Venetian painter lived in Cannaregio and is buried here. The church contains some of his largest and most dramatic canvases (The Last Judgement, The Worship of the Golden Calf) in the setting he intended them for. Entry €3, almost never crowded.

### Castello — Venice Without Filters

The largest sestiere, stretching east from San Marco past the Arsenal to the Giardini. The eastern half is entirely residential — children playing in campos, grocery deliveries by boat, pensioners on benches. No major tourist attractions to concentrate crowds.

**The Arsenale:** Venice's shipyard, established in 1104 and expanded repeatedly through the medieval and Renaissance periods, became the largest industrial complex in the pre-industrial world — at its peak employing 16,000 workers (the "Arsenalotti") and capable of producing a fully equipped war galley per day. Dante used it as the image of Hell in the Inferno ("as in the Arsenal of the Venetians... the pitch boils..."). Only partially accessible to visitors (it houses part of the Venice Biennale biannually), but the exterior gates and the maritime museum nearby are worth visiting.

**Via Garibaldi:** The widest street (actually a filled-in canal) in Venice, lined with everyday shops — butchers, hardware stores, bakeries. The most convincingly normal street in the city.

**San Pietro di Castello:** The former cathedral of Venice (before San Marco took the title in 1807), on an island at the far eastern end of Castello. Almost no tourists come here. The church has works by Veronese and a remarkable 10th-century throne made from an Arab gravestone. Free entry.

### San Polo — The Market Sestiere

**Rialto Market:** Established 1097, continuously operating at the same site since then. Two sections: the **Pescheria** (fish market, operating Tuesday–Saturday mornings until 12:00) and the **Erberia** (fruit and vegetable market). The Pescheria is extraordinary — the Adriatic's catch laid out on marble slabs, including fish varieties rarely seen outside of Venice and the lagoon. The market is the point where the city's cuisine begins; chefs and home cooks arrive from 07:00. Arrive before 09:00 to see it before tourists dominate.

**Santa Maria Gloriosa dei Frari:** Simply "I Frari" — a vast Gothic church (built 1340–1443) containing Titian's greatest altarpiece, the Assunta (Assumption of the Virgin, 1518), and his own tomb. Also here: Canova's monument (marble pyramid), and an extraordinary altarpiece by Giovanni Bellini. One of the genuinely great church interiors in Italy. Entry: €5.

**Scuola Grande di San Rocco:** A confraternity building decorated entirely by Tintoretto, who spent 23 years (1564–1587) creating 60+ paintings for the walls and ceilings. The competition for the first commission was won when Tintoretto presented a finished ceiling painting rather than the required sketch — the other artists refused to judge it as a sketch. The result is the most immersive Tintoretto experience in existence. Entry: €14.

## The Gondola — Reality vs Expectation

The gondola is Venice's most recognisable image and its most misunderstood transport option. It is not a taxi. No Venetian uses it to get anywhere. It is a ceremonial craft — and the experience of 30 minutes gliding through the small calli canals (not the Grand Canal) on a flat-bottomed black wooden boat is unlike anything else in the world.

**Official 2026 rates:** €90 for 30 minutes (up to 5 passengers) during the day. €110 after 19:00. These are regulated municipal tariffs, not negotiable. A gondolier who quotes you €100 is not overcharging — the evening rate is €110.

**What makes the difference:** The route matters. A gondola ride on the Grand Canal passes boats, vaporetti, and tourist crowds. A ride through the secondary and tertiary canals (the rii) — entering shadowed passages where the buildings almost touch overhead, passing under low bridges where a gondolier must crouch, listening to the sound echo — is categorically different. Ask for the smaller canals when you board.

**Shared gondola options:** Tour operators offer shared gondola rides at €25–40 per person in groups of 4–6. Less intimate, but more affordable.

**Gondolier tradition:** Venice's 400 licensed gondoliers are almost all Venetian-born and male (one female gondolier, the first, was licensed in 2010). The profession is typically passed within families. Training takes years. The black uniform (and the boat's black paint) has been mandatory since 1562, when the Venetian Senate ruled that the increasing competition among nobles to have the most elaborate gondola was too ostentatious.

## Cicchetti Culture — The Best Way to Eat in Venice

Cicchetti (chee-KET-tee) is Venice's answer to tapas — small, seasonal, affordable bites served at bacari (traditional wine bars) throughout the day and evening. This is not a tourist activity. It's how Venetians eat and socialise, and it's been the city's primary social food ritual for centuries.

**The tradition:** Maritime workers developed the habit of eating quickly and standing, grabbing a cicheto and an ombra (a small glass of wine, literally "shadow" — possibly referring to the practice of drinking in the shadow of the Campanile) between shifts. The social ritual — moving from bacaro to bacaro, eating, chatting, moving on — became embedded in Venetian life.

**What cicchetti are:** Small preparations served on bread or crostini, or on toothpicks, or in small portions. The classics:

- **Baccalà mantecato:** Whipped salt cod — air-dried cod rehydrated and beaten with olive oil until it becomes a white, airy cream. The quality benchmark for any bacaro: if the baccalà is smooth, hand-whipped, and garlicky without being sharp, the kitchen is good.
- **Sarde in saor:** Sardines fried then marinated in a sweet-sour mixture of onions, pine nuts, raisins, and vinegar. The origin: a preservation technique for sailors on long voyages — the marinade prevented spoilage. The sweet-sour flavour profile reflects Venice's position at the junction of Arab and European trade routes.
- **Polpette:** Small fried meatballs (meat or fish). Common everywhere.
- **Moeche fritte (seasonal):** Soft-shell crabs from the lagoon, fried whole. Available only twice a year (spring and autumn, when the crabs moult their shells). One of the great seasonal delicacies of Venice.
- **Bigoli in salsa:** Thick, rough-textured spaghetti with a sauce of slowly caramelised onions and anchovies. Humble ingredients, extraordinary depth of flavour.

**The ombra de vin:** A small glass (typically 100–150 ml) of local wine — Soave (dry white from Verona), Valpolicella or Bardolino (reds from Lake Garda), Friulano (white from Friuli). €1.50–2.50.

**The Spritz:** Venice's aperitivo drink — prosecco, Aperol or Campari, soda water, and an olive. Omnipresent from 17:00 onward. In Venice, the classic is Aperol Spritz; purists prefer Select (a Venetian bitter liqueur, more complex than Aperol). €3–5 at a local bacaro, €8–12 in San Marco.

**Where to do the cicchetti crawl:**
- **Fondamenta della Misericordia (Cannaregio):** The best street. Multiple bacari in sequence: Paradiso Perduto, Osteria ai Ormesini, Timon.
- **Campo Santa Margherita (Dorsoduro):** Best for students and a younger crowd. Longer hours.
- **Near the Rialto Market:** Bancogiro, Do Mori (one of Venice's oldest, since 1462), Cantina do Spade.
- **Avoid:** Anywhere in or immediately around St Mark's Square — prices are 3–4× higher, quality lower.

## The Lagoon Islands

### Murano

7 minutes by vaporetto from Fondamente Nove (Line 3 or 4). Murano has been Venice's glass-making island since 1291, when the Venetian government ordered all furnaces moved there for fire safety reasons. For centuries the glassmakers were restricted from leaving — their techniques were trade secrets that Venice protected with extraordinary measures (glassmakers who fled were reportedly pursued by state assassins).

The techniques survived: murrina (cross-section cane glass), filigrana (glass threads embedded in clear glass), and sommerso (layers of coloured glass) are still being practised in the same workshops. A glassblowing demonstration — watching a master take a ball of molten glass and transform it in under 5 minutes into a horse, a vase, or a chandelier component — is genuinely extraordinary.

**Avoid the tourist-factory demonstrations near the vaporetto landing.** Walk 200 metres further into the island to the smaller, family-run workshops where the demonstrations are less performative and more craft-focused. Prices for quality pieces: €20–200. Be aware that some "Murano glass" sold in Venice shops is made in Asia — for authenticity, buy only from Murano directly.

**Museo del Vetro (Glass Museum):** Murano's glass history from Roman times to the present. Entry €10.

### Burano

40 minutes by vaporetto (Line 12 from Fondamente Nove). Venice's lacemaking island — and the most colourful place in Italy. The vibrantly painted houses (each one a different primary colour — regulations govern which colours can be used and where) make it one of the most photographed places in all of Italy.

The origin of the colours: fishermen allegedly painted their houses in distinctive bright colours so they could find their way home through the lagoon fog. Burano lace — intricate needlepoint and bobbin lace — was once so valuable that it was purchased by European royalty; the techniques are now maintained by a shrinking number of practitioners.

**Eat lunch in Burano.** Trattoria da Romano (established 1890, Hemingway ate here) and Al Gatto Nero are the classic addresses. Fresh lagoon fish, risotto di gò (from the goby fish, lagoon-specific), risotto di go, and quiet tables on the canal. Much cheaper than Venice.

### Torcello

50 minutes by vaporetto (Line 12 to Burano, then Line T shuttle). The oldest settlement in the Venetian lagoon — founded in the 7th century, it was the dominant island before the shift of population to the Rialto. Now almost empty — perhaps 10 permanent residents — with fields, a canal, and at the end of it, one of the most important Byzantine churches in the world.

**Santa Maria Assunta (Cathedral of Torcello):** Founded 639 AD, rebuilt in the 11th century. The interior contains a Last Judgement mosaic filling the entire west wall — Byzantine iconography in extraordinary condition — and a Madonna and Child mosaic on the apse ceiling of a harrowing emotional simplicity. The contrast between the now-empty island and the scale of this cathedral is one of the most affecting experiences in Italy. Entry €5 (cathedral), €13 combined with the Museo di Torcello.

Hemingway wrote part of Across the River and Into the Trees at the Locanda Cipriani on Torcello, his favoured retreat from Harry's Bar in Venice.

## What to Eat and Drink — Venice's Distinctive Cuisine

Venice's cuisine is inseparable from its geography — a maritime city that traded with the entire known world, with no agricultural hinterland of its own. The results: a repertoire built on preserved fish (baccalà, sardines, anchovies), lagoon seafood (clams, mussels, crab, eel, lagoon shrimp called schie), and ingredients that arrived via the spice route (raisins, pine nuts, cinnamon, saffron in the sweet-sour sarde in saor).

**Must-eat dishes:**

- **Baccalà mantecato** (see cicchetti section — also served as a main course over polenta)
- **Sarde in saor** (see above — best version: made 24 hours ahead so the flavours meld)
- **Bigoli in salsa** (thick spaghetti with onion-anchovy sauce)
- **Risotto al nero di seppia** (black squid ink risotto — the ink turns the rice intensely black and adds a briny, mineral depth)
- **Risi e bisi** (rice and peas — made with the first spring peas from the lagoon islands, traditionally served to the Doge on 25 April, the feast of St Mark)
- **Fegato alla veneziana** (calf's liver with slowly caramelised sweet onions — a sweet-savoury pairing from Venice's Arab trade influence)
- **Moeche fritte** (soft-shell lagoon crabs, seasonal — spring and autumn only)
- **Fritto misto** (mixed fried seafood, lagoon-caught)
- **Tiramisu** (Venice's claim on the origin of tiramisu is contested with Treviso, but it's local regardless — the best versions use savoiardi biscuits, mascarpone, espresso, marsala)

**Budget eating:** Cicchetti at bacari, €1–3 per piece, is the best value in Venice. Tramezzini veneziani (triangular soft crustless sandwiches with various fillings, €2–3 each) at any bar. A bacaro lunch of cicchetti plus ombra: €10–15.

**Where to avoid spending money:** Restaurants in or immediately around Piazza San Marco, along the Riva degli Schiavoni, and on the direct route between the train station and Rialto. A coffee at the famous Caffè Florian in Piazza San Marco (established 1720, Casanova's favourite) costs €9–15 — extraordinary history, tourist pricing, worth one visit for the experience.

## Getting There and Around

**Airport:** Marco Polo International Airport (VCE), on the mainland 12 km from Venice.

**From the airport to Venice:**
- **Alilaguna water ferry:** Direct from the airport dock to various Venice landing stages (Fondamente Nove, San Zaccaria near San Marco, Rialto, Piazzale Roma). Duration 70–90 minutes, €15. Slow but scenic and direct to the heart of the city.
- **ACTV bus to Piazzale Roma:** €1.50, 20 minutes to the bus terminal at the entrance to Venice. Then vaporetto or walk.
- **Water taxi:** €110–140 direct to any Venice landing stage. 30 minutes. Expensive but direct and private.

**Vaporetto (water bus):**
- Single ticket: €9.50 (valid 75 minutes)
- 24h pass: €25 | 48h: €35 | 72h: €45
- 7-day: €65
- Line 1: Full Grand Canal route, stops at every landing — slow, scenic, essential at least once
- Line 2: Faster Grand Canal route, fewer stops
- Lines 3, 4: To Murano
- Line 12: To Burano and Torcello (from Fondamente Nove)

**On foot:** Most major sights are 20–25 minutes' walk from each other. The city is compact. The confusion is in the maze of small calli — get comfortable with Google Maps for pedestrian navigation.

## Acqua Alta — Venice Flooding, Explained

Acqua alta (high water) is the periodic flooding of Venice's low-lying areas. San Marco is the lowest point in the city and floods most frequently.

**The MOSE barriers (Modulo Sperimentale Elettromeccanico):** Completed after decades of delays and corruption scandals, the MOSE mobile flood barriers were fully operational from 2023. They consist of 78 hinged metal flaps on the three inlets connecting the lagoon to the Adriatic — raised when exceptionally high tides are forecast, physically separating the lagoon from the sea. Since full operation, the frequency and severity of major acqua alta events has dramatically reduced.

**What to do if acqua alta occurs:** The city deploys raised wooden walkways (passerelle) through flooded areas. Waterproof boot covers (soprascarpe) are sold everywhere for €5–10. San Marco can be waded through with them. The phenomenon has become less a disaster and more a weather event to dress for.

## Practical Tips

**The city is best early morning and evening.** The crowds arrive around 10:00 and peak 11:00–16:00. Before 09:00, St Mark's Square has almost no tourists — just pigeons and delivery boats. After 18:00, most day-trippers have left, the light turns extraordinary, and the city becomes navigable.

**Don't eat near San Marco.** The economic gradient in Venice is the steepest of any European city — 200 metres from the square, prices halve. Eat in Dorsoduro, Cannaregio, Castello, or San Polo.

**Your navigation app will send you the wrong way sometimes.** Venice's calli don't follow any grid. When the app says "turn left in 20 metres," there may be a canal with no bridge for 100 metres in that direction. Build extra time into walking estimates.

**Mestre vs Venice for accommodation:** Mestre is the mainland city adjacent to Venice — hotels are dramatically cheaper (€50–80 vs €180–300). The trade-off: you miss the experience of Venice in the evening when most tourists have left and the city becomes itself. If budget allows, stay in Venice. If not, Mestre plus an early start is entirely viable.

**Large suitcases in Venice:** Every bridge has steps. A rolling suitcase over 150+ bridges per day is exhausting — and will annoy every local you encounter (the noise on stone bridges is considerable). Bring the most compact luggage you can manage.
